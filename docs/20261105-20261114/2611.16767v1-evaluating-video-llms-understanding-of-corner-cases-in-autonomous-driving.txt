Abstract
Natural language explanations promised by modern Video Large Language Models
(Video LLMs) offer autonomous driving systems a path toward detailed scene description
and interpretable decision making on the road. While recent benchmarks have begun as-
sessing these models on driving scenes, they mainly target routine traffic conditions and
general comprehension tasks based on existing driving datasets and therefore fail to eval-
uate model performance on the rarest and most safety-critical driving events, also known
as "corner cases". To provide a comprehensive evaluation of Video LLMs on precisely
these scenarios, we introduce DashBench, a benchmark testing 25 Video LLMs on 1,709
carefully curated driving videos spanning not only collision-related corner cases but also
rare non-collision ones. Through extensive prompts and open-ended question answering,
we expose a critical capability gap: all evaluated models perform substantially below the
threshold required for safety-critical driving applications, frequently misjudging or miss-
ing dangerous events on the road ahead and hallucinating non-existent agents or actions
in their explanations. We believe DashBench, combining an extensive dataset with a
rigorous evaluation toolkit and a detailed diagnosis of current model limitations, drives
progress toward safer, more explainable autonomous driving systems. The code and data
will be shared at: cornercase-dashbench.github.io

Introduction
Autonomous driving systems (ADS) face a fundamental transparency barrier: the learned
models that process continuous dashcam video streams and translate perception into plan-
ning decisions [8] generate no real-time account of their reasoning, yet real-time natural-
language explanations of individual maneuvers are increasingly recognised as an operational
necessity: they support passenger awareness, enable operator oversight, and are a decisive
factor in how drivers calibrate their reliance on the system [13, 61]. Video Large Language
Models (Video LLMs), multimodal models capable of reasoning over video inputs and gen-
erating grounded language descriptions have emerged as the leading candidate to close this
interpretability gap in autonomous driving (AD). Video LLMs have been adapted for driving-
specific scene comprehension, safety-critical event understanding, and language-grounded
planning [9, 22]; leading commercial ADS have begun incorporating them for real-time nar-
ration [26, 51, 52]. Whether Video LLMs can fulfil this promise under the most demanding
road conditions which are rare, safety-critical events far outside routine traffic which are re-
ferred to as corner cases [2, 4], is a question made especially urgent by a possible failure
mode of Video LLMs. These models do not merely underperform on hard scenes, they can
hallucinate and systematically omit important details from their descriptions [27], producing
active misinformation precisely where accurate perception matters most [42].
Although there have been several works probing Video LLMs’ scene understanding in
corner cases for AD, they present several distinguishable limitations: (1) Insufficient cov-
erage of corner case diversity. Existing benchmarks in this domain [7, 53, 59] restrict
themselves either to collision-related events [59] or to object-level corner case detection
[7], leaving scenario-level non-collision hazards largely untested [4]. (2) Limitations of
experimental design and evaluation metrics. DVBench [59], the benchmark most closely
related to ours, relies solely on a multiple-choice question (MCQ) format, narrowing eval-
uation to option-picking and providing limited evidence of the model’s genuine perception
and explanatory reasoning [25]; subsequent work has highlighted the need for nuanced au-

tomatic judges to evaluate free-text responses in AD scenarios [53]. (3) Lack of evaluation
of driving-specialized Video LLMs on corner case scenarios. Existing benchmarks that do
evaluate models on driving corner cases assess general-purpose models exclusively and do
not include driving-specialist Video LLMs [22, 32] that are explicitly designed and fine-
tuned for driving video understanding. Whether such driving-specialized models close the
corner-case reasoning gap over general-purpose Video LLMs remains an unanswered and
practically important question, especially for guiding the development of road-ready Video
LLMs.
We present DashBench, the first benchmark to not only systematically stress-test Video
LLMs on corner case scenarios on the road under a multi-prompt evaluation protocol but
also investigate the challenges presented by our task through anomaly-localized experiments
and hallucination mitigation efforts in order to gain deeper insights. Our contributions are:
1. We curate, annotate, and process a dataset of 1,709 dashcam-video clips spanning
88 categories explicitly balanced across collision-related and non-collision hazardous
events. As shown in Figure 1, our dataset covers a broad array of scenario-level events.
2. We evaluate 25 Video LLMs including SOTA closed-source models, open-source
models, and driving-specialist Video LLMs fine-tuned on AD datasets, and find a con-
sistent capability gap: even the highest-scoring model reaches only 46.52% accuracy,
well below any threshold that safety-critical deployment would demand. Notably,
model design such as reasoning and driving-specialist fine-tuning yields no clear, con-
sistent advantage over general-purpose models on corner-case scenarios.
3. We document systematic failure modes through qualitative analysis of model outputs
which are corner-case blindness (the model produces a fluent, confident description
that entirely ignores the hazard) and road agent/action hallucination (non-existent
vehicles, objects, or actions of other agents are fabricated). As shown in Figures 3,
4, and 5, these failures are not isolated errors but structural patterns that recur across
model families.
4. We demonstrate through extensive experiments that neither varying prompt formula-
tions nor providing temporally localized corner case windows (isolating the frames
where the hazard occurs) substantially improves corner-case reasoning, with perfor-
mance gains remaining well below what safety-critical driving applications require.
Additionally, applying existing hallucination mitigation techniques yields no consis-
tent gains on safety-critical scenarios. Together, these findings suggest that closing the
corner-case gap requires fundamentally new approaches.

Related Works
2.1
Open-Ended QA with Video LLMs
Closed-set, multiple-choice video-QA formats [33] are susceptible to inflating apparent
model capability by rewarding answer-option pattern matching [11], motivating a shift to
open-ended, free-text evaluation. Video-MME [16] evaluates Video LLMs across diverse
domains and task types and shows that state-of-the-art models fall substantially below
human-level performance, while TemporalBench [5] specifically targets fine-grained tem-
poral understanding and finds similarly large gaps. Beyond format, evaluation is sensitive to

prompt design: the same question phrased differently substantially affects output quality and
can reverse rankings between models [15]. Reliable capability assessment of Video LLMs

2.2
Benchmarking Video LLMs for Corner Cases in AD
Early benchmarks for driving video understanding evaluate Video LLMs exclusively on rou-
tine traffic conditions [34], without systematic coverage of safety-critical events. This gap
was formalized by [7], establishing CODA-LM as the first benchmark to probe VLMs on
corner cases, though limited to static, object-level anomalies. DVBench [59] evaluated 14
Video LLMs on 10K crash and near-crash MCQs, finding that no model exceeded 40% accu-
racy, yet the closed-set format could not provide deeper insights on whether failures stemmed
from perceptual blindness, reasoning errors, or output formatting. CrashSight [18] broadens
this further to roadside-camera footage with phase-aware reasoning tasks. Despite these
advances, all existing corner-case benchmarks remain narrow, covering either object-level
anomalies or crash-only events, and none systematically characterize the types of perceptual
errors that drive underperformance. No benchmark to date provides diverse scenario-level
coverage with open-ended, multi-prompt free-text scoring.
3
DashBench
We present DashBench, a benchmark of 1,709 ego-view dashcam video clips designed to
evaluate Video LLMs on scenario-level corner cases in autonomous driving, spanning 88
corner case categories drawn from two complementary sources: MM-AU [14] and RoadSo-
cial [39], with a deliberate balance between collision and non-collision scenarios. Unless
otherwise stated, 16 frames are uniformly sampled per clip as a standardized multi-frame

evaluation protocol; benchmark construction is described thoroughly in this section, and the
experimental setup is presented in Section 4.
3.1
Motivation
Driving corner cases are broadly defined as rare and unexpected situations that deviate from
normal traffic behavior, challenging the reliability of autonomous perception systems [2, 3,
4]. Following the scenario-level corner case taxonomy in [3], DashBench targets scenario-
level corner-cases which are well-suited for Video LLM evaluation since recognition of these
events requires temporal reasoning over a video sequence. Within this level, the taxonomy
separates anomalous, novel, and risky scenarios primarily by whether they carry collision po-
tential. Building on this, our distinct dataset design splits clips by collision outcome (which
is whether physical impact occurred). Collision clips capture scenarios in which the hazard
resulted in actual impact; non-collision clips span the remaining range, from averted dangers
and near-misses to unusual but safe road events.
We build DashBench’s primary video corpus from MM-AU, the most fine-grained pub-
licly available ego-view dashcam accident benchmark with 57 annotated categories and
keyframe annotations. However, dashcam footage is predominantly collected for collision-
related accident documentation [6] and datasets curated from such footage therefore system-
atically over-represent collision events. Out of MM-AU’s 57 categories, 46 describe direct
collision events, yielding 9,142 collision samples against only 626 non-collision samples
which is a ratio of approximately 14.6:1 and a collision share of 93.6% of all annotated
clips. Underrepresented non-collision categories include vehicle loss of control, traffic rule
violations without impact, and various road surface and environmental hazards, even though
these are scenarios which are safety-critical as well. Therefore, a benchmark constructed
solely from MM-AU would cover almost exclusively scenarios in which collision potential
resulted in actual impact, leaving other real-world safety-critical events that do not end in
collision absent from evaluation.
Therefore, to achieve broad scenario-level coverage, we augment the samples we will
select from MM-AU with non-collision corner case clips from RoadSocial, a large-scale
social-media road event dataset spanning varied geographies, camera perspectives, and road
event types. This dataset captures a wide spectrum of non-collision events absent from typi-
cal accident-focused datasets, and its video-level descriptive tags provide a principled basis
for mapping clips to standardized corner case categories. Though we do not claim exhaustive
coverage of all possible corner cases as no finite collection of videos can enumerate all pos-
sible real-world road hazards [4], we believe that our constructed dataset provides the most
wide-ranging and balanced ego-view collection yet assembled for scenario-level corner case
evaluation, spanning 88 different categories.
3.2
Dataset Composition
As established in our motivation, non-collision corner cases are severely underrepresented in
existing ego-view dashcam datasets; to address this gap and achieve balanced scenario-level
coverage, DashBench is composed of two sources. From MM-AU, we first retain all 626
non-collision clips spanning 11 categories (including vehicle loss of control and component
failures); although MM-AU already provides ego-view footage and keyframe annotations,
each clip still undergoes manual inspection to confirm that the corner case is visually identi-
fiable and correctly categorized, and to refine keyframe boundaries where needed. To extend

coverage beyond these 11 categories, we additionally draw on RoadSocial, comprising of
13.2K social-media road event videos across different camera viewpoints, each associated
with descriptive tags out of a vocabulary of 21,325 video-level tags. Filtering and prepar-
ing these clips required three steps: an LLM-assisted pipeline mapping video-level tags to
candidate corner case categories to identify relevant clips, a check to ensure clips exhibit
ego-view dashcam perspective, and manual verification that a corner case is visually present,
with annotators additionally labeling corner case keyframes (frame at the start of the corner
case, the frame at which it is most observable, and the frame marking its end). This selection
and annotation process was carried out by five annotators over four months, and the com-
plete protocol is described in Figure 2. Through this pipeline, RoadSocial yields 314 clips
covering 31 new corner case categories.
For the collision-side subset, we sample from MM-AU’s 9,142 collision clips across
all 46 collision categories, targeting a category-balanced selection matching the size of the
constructed non-collision subset. MM-AU provides keyframe annotations for the corner
cases in all clips, but as previously mentioned, we manually refine keyframe boundaries
where needed (cases where the keyframe does not exactly show the start, end, or the most
observable frame of the corner case event).
To ensure that all clips are evaluable under the 16-frame uniform sampling protocol,
we apply two quality criteria across all candidate clips from both sources. First, we ensure
that the corner case must be self-contained (not requiring additional context outside of the
images, e.g. sound/narration). Second, the corner case must be unambiguously identifiable
from the 16 uniformly sampled frames. The final DashBench benchmark comprises 1,709
clips across 88 corner case categories. The average length for all clips in our benchmark
is 7.70s while the average length of the corner case window for all clips is 3.47s. The full
category list along with sample counts are provided in Table 5 in the Appendix.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setups
DashBench is an open-ended, free-text response benchmark. While early video-QA datasets
often relied on fixed multiple-choice formats, recent studies highlight that these multiple-
choice tasks allow models to exploit shortcuts, revealing little about genuine multimodal
reasoning or visual grounding [25, 29]. As a result, recent benchmark efforts have shifted
toward open-ended evaluation to properly stress-test Video LLMs’ understanding of time-
sensitive and knowledge-intensive visual events [12, 45]. Figure 3 shows the concise pipeline
of experiments in our dataset, from how we obtain both a model’s response and a ground truth
description to then subsequently pass them to DeepSeekV3-as-a-judge for evaluation.
4.1.1
Prompts
For each sample, we query the Video LLM with five prompts:
(Q1) What is unusual in this sequence of frames?
(Q2) What is dangerous in this sequence of frames?
(Q3) Describe this sequence of frames in detail.
(Q4) Asks the model to return a single JSON object with two fields:
(a) (Q4TF) – true if there is an unusual/dangerous event and false if it is a normal
road situation
(b) (Q4R) – free-text reasoning that justifies the answer in (Q4TF)
(Q5) First gives a definition of a corner case scenario along with some examples. Then,
asks the model to identify if there is any corner case and to describe it.
Q1–Q2 are designed as explicit questions to force the model to search for anomalies,
using both unusual and dangerous to cover diverse wording that can be used to describe
corner cases. Q3 takes a different approach by asking for a general scene description with no
explicit mention of danger, anomalies, or corner cases. We consider this a critical test since
a Video LLM that truly understands the unfolding scene should identify the evident corner
case naturally, without any textual hint to guide it. Q4 serves as an integrated detection-
and-explanation prompt where we jointly check two aspects: (i) Q4TF, the binary question
format is designed to check whether the model might be sensitive to the wording in the
prompt, and (ii) in Q4R we ask the Video LLM to give its reasoning as to what factors in
the images display a routine traffic scene or corner case on the road. This "reasoning" test
examines the Video LLM’s visual grounding capability and whether it guesses in Q4TF due
to the prompt’s nature. Since the format of Q4 requires the model to return JSON outputs, we
could not provide Q4 results on several models since they cannot do this consistently. Lastly,
Q5 introduces a definition-guided setting: we provide the model with a description of what a
corner case is as well as some examples, then direct it to identify and describe any such event
in the clip. Video-QA benchmarks generally avoid including in-context examples directly in
the prompt [29, 34, 44], as video inputs already consume a large context budget and make
adding examples computationally expensive. Q5 therefore stays within this convention by

4.1.3
Evaluation
To reliably evaluate the alignment between Video LLM responses and ground truth annota-
tions, we employ the LLM-as-judge evaluation protocol [63]. This has become the standard
practice for open-ended benchmarks, with recent work employing GPT variants [7, 34],
DeepSeekV3 [43], and Llama3.1(70B) [31] as evaluators. In our setup, the LLM-as-
judge assesses Q1–Q3 and Q4R using a structured binary judgment prompt that asks whether
each model response captures the same corner case as the ground truth in terms of its cause,
the agents responsible, and event timing. This criterion counts only responses receiving a Yes
verdict toward the accuracy score, ensuring a metric that reflects semantic alignment, while
Q4TF is reported separately as an independent binary detection score. We validate LLM-as-
judge selection by randomly sampling 100 responses of several representative Video LLMs
and comparing each candidate judge’s verdicts on those responses to actual human judg-
ments using Cohen’s kappa (κ) [10] as the inter-rater agreement measure. As shown in
Table 2, DeepSeekV3 achieves the highest agreement with human judgments (κ = 0.96)
among all candidates tested, including Llama3.1(70B) [35] and a multi-LLM ensemble
based on [48] (the details for this ensemble setup are explained in Appendix D), so we select
DeepSeekV3 as our LLM-as-judge. As an open-source model from a different family than
the evaluated Video LLMs, DeepSeekV3 additionally supports reproducible evaluation and
avoids circular self-evaluation bias [38]. Furthermore, to verify that our evaluation scheme
is not sensitive to stochastic variation, we run the DeepSeekV3-as-a-judge evaluation five
times on a representative set of model responses; the mean accuracy and standard deviation
across all prompts and models confirm consistent evaluation (std < 1% in all cases). The

Corner-Case Understanding Remains Challenging Across All Model Types and Scale.
Among all models, GPT-5 achieves the highest Q4R accuracy at a lacking 46.52% and an
average agreement of 34.75%, while Gemini-3-Flash follows closely at 34.41% aver-
age. Though these numbers remain far below any practical safety threshold, the lead over the
strongest open-source model Qwen3-VL (235B, 20.67% avg), still exceeds 14% in average
agreement (34.75% vs. 20.67%), suggesting that our task remains challenging even as open-
source general multimodal capabilities continue to improve. Notably, scaling model size
within the same model family yields only modest and inconsistent gains. While scaling to
extremely large open-source models (235B+) does improve performance, the gains remain
surprisingly modest. For example, Qwen3-VL (235B) achieves only a ∼2% higher average
agreement than its 8B counterpart (20.67% vs. 18.48%). Similarly, scaling InternVL3.5
from 8B to 241B improves average agreement by only ∼7.43% (8.64% vs. 16.07%). These
results suggest that simply increasing model scale alone is insufficient for robust understand-
ing of rare and safety-critical driving corner cases.
Neither Reasoning nor Driving Specialization Solves Corner-Case Understanding.
Additionally, neither extended chain-of-thought reasoning nor domain-specific driving
specialization appears sufficient for robust corner-case understanding. Reasoning-oriented
models such as Ovis2.5, KeyeVL, and GLM-4.1V-Thinking show no clear advan-
tage over similarly sized open-source Video LLMs, as Ovis2.5 reaches 16.43% average,
behind the non-reasoning Qwen3-VL at 18.48%, while GLM-4.1V-Thinking scores
only 6.33% despite its thinking mode. In particular, specialist driving Video LLMs achieve
at most 10.01% average agreement despite being explicitly fine-tuned on AD datasets
and for AD tasks, with several models collapsing to near-random performance.
This
suggests that current specialist training strategies may encourage shortcut learning tied to
benchmark-specific patterns rather than genuine understanding of complex driving events.
Video LLMs Depend Heavily on Linguistic Cueing Rather Than Robust Visual Under-
standing.
Variations in our prompts’ wording reveal that model performance can be highly
sensitive to semantic framing: the same scene queried under different linguistic formulations
shows score swings of up to 26% within a single model (e.g., 19.95% on Q1 to 46.52% on
Q4R for GPT-5), a critical consideration for prompt design when deploying Video LLMs in
driving scenarios. Additionally, through Q4R and Q4TF, we reveal the importance of free-
text reasoning: are Video LLMs’ grounded explanations on successfully detecting a corner
case valid? All evaluated models suffer a sharp drop in performance when asked to explain
their decisions. On average, accuracy falls by 55 percentage points between Q4TF and Q4R.
This insight calls for the integration of additional modules that verify visual evidence be-

Discussion and Conclusion
Our results reveal that the latest Video LLMs, regardless of open vs. proprietary status,
model scale, reasoning capability, or fine-tuning on established driving datasets, fall criti-
cally short when tasked with understanding corner cases. No evaluated model surpasses 50%
on any individual prompt, and none exceeds 35% on average: GPT-5, the highest performer,
reaches only 46.52% on Q4R and 34.75% average agreement. Open-source models are fur-
ther behind still, with the strongest open-source result (Qwen3-VL 235B, 20.67% average)
lagging the closed-source best by over 14 percentage points. When considering model train-
ing recipes neither extended chain-of-thought reasoning nor domain-specific driving special-
ization on existing datasets narrows this gap, as specialist models fine-tuned on AD datasets
achieve at most 10.01% average agreement, with several performing near chance. More crit-
ically, all Video LLMs frequently hallucinate nonexistent road agents, fabricate imaginary
actions, or misclassify collisions as near-misses in addition to simply missing the evident
corner case on the road. These failure modes direct safety risks when Video LLMs are em-
bedded as explainability or planning components in an ADS.
Our benchmark design and additional experiments conducted further expose the depth of
the problem. Prompt formulation is far from a trivial design choice when deploying Video
LLMs for road scene analysis, as the expectation that models can robustly describe scenes
without being cued to look for anomalies does not hold. We also explored whether narrowing
the temporal context to the anomalous event itself could help: isolating the corner-case win-
dow does benefit stronger models by several percentage points (+3.79 pp for GPT-5), but
the gains for open-source models are negligible (+0.81–1.98 pp), ruling out insufficient vi-
sual context as the primary obstacle. We further attempt to address these failures by applying
three recent hallucination mitigation methods,with the best approach gaining only +1.16 pp
on average. Together, these findings indicate that closing the corner-case gap requires fun-
damentally new approaches: future Video LLM assistants and planners should demonstrate
robust visual grounding and must be exposed to diverse scenarios during training. Addition-
ally, motivated by the scarcity of rare non-collision corner cases in naturalistic footage, we
believe that the development of driving-domain world models that can synthesize diverse,
controllable safety-critical scenarios is a promising direction for future dataset construction.
DashBench provides the most comprehensive ego-view corner-case evaluation of Video
LLMs to date: 1,709 dashcam clips across 88 fine-grained categories, balanced across colli-
sion and non-collision events, with open-ended free-text QA evaluated by a reliable, SOTA
LLM-as-a-judge. Together with a detailed characterization of systematic failure modes and
a suite of diagnostic experiments, we offer both a rigorous stress test and a practical guide
for the limitations that must be overcome. We believe DashBench serves as a foundational
resource for advancing Video LLMs toward the perceptual robustness and safety-critical re-
liability that trustworthy autonomous driving demands.

C
Ground Truth Annotation
C.1
Assisted Scene Annotation with o3
Since writing detailed scene descriptions for hundreds of clips is labour-intensive, we lever-
age OpenAI’s o3 model to generate detailed scene-level annotations. The process for gen-
erating initial ground truth annotations using o3’s assistance for real corner case scenarios
involve these steps:
Keyframe extraction For each clip, we extract three critical keyframes representing the
progression of the anomalous event. These frames correspond to: (1) the start of the video,
(2) the corner case occurrence frame (when the scene reaches its most critical point), and
(3) end of the video. This temporal breakdown allows the model to observe the evolution of
each incident, though with a limited context.
Contextual prompting Along with the keyframes, we provide the corner-case category
label as a concise answer to the model. This helps the model ground its scene-level annota-
tion based on the given category answer, encouraging it to focus on relevant visual semantics
and avoid generic captions.
Scene description generation Using these inputs, the o3 model generates descriptive
captions as prompted. The detailed prompt can be seen in Figure 6. These captions capture
both object-level details and event-level dynamics.

C.2
Manual Annotation Review and Validation
However, even with passed keyframes and the descriptive hint from the corner case category
for creating the ground truth annotation, o3 might fail to create a descriptive annotation
based on what actually happened in the scene since its context is limited and it might misin-
terpret key details. To ensure accuracy and reliability of automatically generated annotations,
we implement a multi-phase validation pipeline involving human annotators. This process
verifies that all labels and descriptions reflect the visual evidence, align with intended se-
mantics, and maintain consistency across the dataset.
Validation pipeline As shown in Figure 10, our annotation refinement pipeline follows
a systematic approach:
1. Initial manual review All scene descriptions generated by o3 are first reviewed by a
human annotator. The annotator checks for factual errors, hallucinations, inconsisten-
cies, or ambiguities in the captions, by comparing them with the full list of frames for
the specific sample. With the full context, minor phrasing issues or missing details
are corrected at this stage to enhance clarity and precision.
2. Evaluator panel verification After initial manual revision, a panel of three evaluators
examines the refined annotations and also compares with the full list of frames for the
specific sample. Each evaluator independently assesses whether the caption accurately
and adequately describes the visual content and evolving corner case situation in the
specific scenario.
3. Unanimous agreement check We adopt a strict unanimity principle for final ground-
truth acceptance. The revised ground truth annotation is approved only if all three
evaluators independently agree on its validity. This high consensus bar ensures ro-
bustness and minimizes individual bias or interpretation errors.
4. Conflict resolution If full agreement is not reached, the sample is flagged as "in con-
flict." These cases are brought back to the initial human annotator for additional human
revision and will pass through the evaluator panel again.
5. Final ground truth consolidation Once consensus is achieved through unanimous
agreement, the validated ground truth annotation is finalized. These annotations form
the basis of the benchmark’s evaluation targets, ensuring trustworthiness and repro-
ducibility for future research. An example corner case scenario with the initial o3-
generated initial annotation and the revised, final ground-truth annotation is shown in
Figure 11.

VDGD (Visual Description-Grounded Decoding)
VDGD [20] uses description-prefixing, operating in two sequential passes over the same 16

priors. No contrastive branch is required.

sequence, suppressing responses that could be generated from a single static frame alone.

• alpha (contrastive weight): 0.5
• Positive branch: all 16 original frames
• Negative branch: single middle frame (paths[len(paths)//2])
• Number of branches: 2 (full / partial)

Additional Qualitative Examples
G.1
Failing to Identify Evident Corner Cases
Despite being evident in the sequence of frames, we show that Video LLMs regardless of
proprietary status, size, and whether it was fine-tuned on driving data fails to identify obvi-
ous corner cases, instead focusing on other parts of the road, as shown by the response of
CrashChat on Figure 12 and Gemini-3-Flash on Figure 16.
G.2
Hallucinating On the Road
Figures 13 shows a case where Video LLMs hallucinate road agents or objects on the road,
which are simply not there. For possible deployment as part of a vision-language-action
framework enabler, Video LLMs need to overcome this glaring issue of hallucinating on the
road.
G.3
Collisions as Near-Misses
Genuine collisions on the road are important corner cases because they might affect subse-
quent actions that will be taken by the ADS based on the Video LLM’s observation ; treating
them as near-misses due to failing to localize the exact moment where a collision occurs
(possibly due to the large number of frames passed) presents a severe issue that should be
addressed. Figure 14 shows this occurrence.
G.4
Making Up Actions on the Road
Another concerning point for Video LLMs as future co-navigators and planners is if they
invent or misinterpret the actions of other road agents (e.g., a swerving car when in reality
the car is driving straight). Figure 15 shows a case where the Video LLMs’ hallucination of
actions and behavior can lead to different crucial decisions made in that scenario.
