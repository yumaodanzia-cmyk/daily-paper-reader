## Abstract 

High-fidelity LiDAR simulation remains a bottleneck for scalable development and validation of autonomous sys-tems. Existing generators can synthesize realistic scans, but they are typically constrained to a single sensor con-figuration, offer limited control over structure and geome-try, and drift in long-horizon 4D generation. We present CrossLiDAR, a unified transformer-based framework capa-ble of generating controllable and spatio-temporally con-sistent multi-sensor LiDAR sequences. Our approach lever-ages a rectified flow objective with a Multi-Modal Dif-fusion Transformer (MM-DiT) backbone, within a com-pact latent space of range images. To support multi-ple LiDAR sensors, an Inter-sensor Module enables in-teraction across sensors, while a ControlNet-style branch provides structural conditioning (e.g., BEV priors or 3D bounding boxes). For modeling the temporal consistency, CrossLiDAR generates short N -frame sequences and ex-tends them autoregressively to improve long-horizon coher-ence. Our model significantly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving an average improvement of 29.56% in JSD and 15.36% in MMD evaluated on nuScenes and the Waymo Dataset. CrossLiDAR bridges the gap to modern autonomous-driving simulation by enabling calibration-consistent multi-sensor LiDAR rollouts over long horizons for controllable, scenario-based testing.

Furthermore, cap-turing critical “corner cases” [71], such as high-impact col-lisions or extreme meteorological conditions, remains in-herently dangerous and rare in physical datasets [55]. Traditional simulation toolkits [7, 20, 30, 33] require labor-intensive manual scene modeling or existing real-world scans, limiting their scalability. Therefore, research has shifted toward generative models to automate this pro-cess, despite the inherent challenges of LiDAR data: high sparsity, non-uniformity, and intrinsic geometric structure. Early generators built on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [3, 12] and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) [17] often produced “fuzzy” geometries and detail loss. While score-based models like LiDARGen [74] im-arXiv:2611.11131v1 [cs.CV] 8 Nov 2026 proved fidelity, they suffered from slow sampling speed and poor long-range performance. More recently, UltraL-iDAR [61] utilized VQ-VAEs [51] in the Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) to enhance realism. However, processing sparse BEV representations often results in significant computa-tional waste on empty voxels, leading to inefficient genera-tion. Alternatively, RangeLDM [13] and LiDMs [41] lever-age Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs) [43] in the range-view to improve speed. However, these frameworks are primarily restricted to fixed sensor architectures and in-put dimensions, preventing cross-sensor scalability. Fur-thermore, they lack fine-grained structural guidance (e.g., ControlNet [70]), rendering them impractical for real-world usage, where side LiDAR proves to be more affordable. More recently, LiDARCrafter [22] extended LiDAR gen-eration to 4D scene modeling by employing a tri-branch diffusion framework that generates temporally ordered 3D LiDAR sequences, with time serving as the fourth dimen-sion. While it achieves impressive temporal consistency, the model remains limited to single-LiDAR sensor configu-rations. In modern autonomous driving architectures, how-ever, configurations with multiple LiDAR sensors have be-come the industry standard [49]. This shift is driven by the move toward cost-effective sensor redundancy, where multiple low-profile units augment or replace a single high-performance rooftop scanner to eliminate blind spots. Un-like single-sensor setups, these distributed arrays introduce non-trivial geometric disparities and complex overlapping fields of view. Existing 4D models typically lack the archi-tectural mechanisms to perform cross-sensor fusion. To address these limitations, we introduce CrossLiDAR, a rectified flow model [26] that departs from conventional U-Net-based architectures [44] in favor of a transformer-based design: the Multi-Modal Diffusion Transformer (MM-DiT) [9], see Figure 1. By leveraging the supe-rior modeling capacity and flexibility of transformers [52], CrossLiDAR captures the intricate geometric dependencies of LiDAR sequences with significantly higher fidelity than previous methods. Central to our framework is a special-ized Inter-sensor Module designed to decouple the genera-tion process from specific hardware constraints. This mod-ule enables our model to handle multiple LiDAR sensors in parallel, modeling their interactions and ensuring geometric consistency, without the need to design separate models for each sensor configuration. This is particularly effective for the increasingly common integration of low-cost side sen-sors. To provide more fine-grained geometric consistency, we incorporate a ControlNet-style branch, which allows for precise scene manipulation through various priors, includ-ing 3D bounding boxes and BEV maps [40]. Finally, to achieve high-fidelity temporal LiDAR generation, CrossLi-DAR introduces a LiDAR Sequence Module . Unlike exist-ing methods that focus solely on single-frame generation, our model performs multi-frame generation (e.g., 5-frame sequence). These short-term sequences can then be itera-tively extended through an autoregressive mechanism, en-abling the synthesis of long-horizon [34], temporally stable LiDAR sequences. To the best of our knowledge, CrossL-iDAR serves as the first truly controllable and multi-sensor pipeline for LiDAR simulation. We evaluate CrossLiDAR on two large-scale datasets: Waymo Open Dataset [49], which provides the multi-sensor data from peripheral side LiDARs, and nuScenes [4]. Compared to existing baselines, in the image-based setup, CrossLiDAR achieves an average improvement of 29.56% in JSD and 15.36% in MMD, with particularly superior per-formance in the multi-sensor configurations. 

Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We introduce CrossLiDAR, a unified diffusion trans-former that enables multi-sensor LiDAR generation via an Inter-sensor Module , and supports controllable syn-thesis from geometric cues through a ControlNet branch. • We present a 4D generation strategy that jointly synthe-sizes short multi-frame sequences with a LiDAR Sequence Module and extends them autoregressively, reducing tem-poral drift and improving long-horizon consistency. • We show state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on Waymo (multi-sensor) and nuScenes (single-sensor) for both single-frame generation and LiDAR sequences. 

## 2. Related Work and Background 

Multisensor LiDAR Generation. Generative models for autonomous driving assume a single LiDAR sensor, whereas real vehicles often deploy a heterogeneous suite (e.g., top-mounted long-range and multiple side-mounted sensors) to capture overlapping perspectives. Multi-sensor data generation has been extensively explored in the camera domain, where recent works target the consistent synthesis of multi-camera surround-view [11, 21, 54, 64]. In con-trast, to our knowledge, no prior generative model explic-itly leverages multiple LiDAR sensors simultaneously. Akey technical challenge is that multi-LiDAR point clouds must be carefully aligned: sensors at different positions yield point clouds in distinct coordinate frames. Unless corrected, naively merging multi-LiDAR data leads to dis-tributional artifacts and geometric inconsistencies [13, 59]. Existing LiDAR generators, including LiDARGen [74], RangeLDM [13], and LiDARCrafter [22], operate on a single aggregated point cloud per scene without explicitly addressing sensor-specific geometries. Recent range- and layout-based methods broaden LiDAR generation beyond unconditional scan synthesis. SPIRAL [73] jointly gen-erates range-view depth, reflectance, and semantic maps, while La La LiDAR [27] introduces scene-graph and lay-out conditioning for controllable object-level LiDAR scene generation. Veila [28] generates panoramic LiDAR from a monocular RGB image, and DynamicCity [1] models dy-namic scenes through 4D occupancy generation. However, these methods do not explicitly model synchronized, cali-brated multi-LiDAR sensors. Furthermore, other works us-ing the Waymo Open Dataset [49], one of the few avail-able multi-sensor datasets, often restrict themselves to only the front-facing sensor [8, 60, 63, 72]. As a result, there is a need for dedicated architectures employing inter-sensor modules, which maintain the unique geometric properties of individual sensors while ensuring global spatial consistency across the entire sensor suite. 

LiDAR Generation Architectures. Beyond sensor con-figuration, the architectural foundations of LiDAR gener-ative models are rapidly evolving. Previous latent diffu-sion models (LDMs), such as RangeLDM [13], LiDM [41], and LiDARCrafter [22], rely on conventional 2D convolu-tional U-Net backbones. These architectures are often con-strained by local receptive fields, which can struggle to cap-ture the global context and long-range dependencies inher-ent in sparse 3D data. To address these structural bottle-necks, MM-DiT [9] architectures offer a more scalable so-lution. By leveraging self-attention, transformer-based ap-proaches capture spatial and temporal dependencies more effectively than traditional U-Net-based frameworks. 

LiDAR Sequence Generation. Point cloud prediction [31, 56–58] has emerged as a fundamental self-supervised task in autonomous driving, utilizing historical sequences to forecast future spatial states. Early methods focused on es-timating scene flow between consecutive frames to project future point cloud sequences [25, 68]. However, these flow-based approaches incur substantial computational costs, limiting their practical applicability in real-time systems. Consequently, recent studies have shifted toward direct prediction from raw point cloud data [29, 37, 56, 57] to improve efficiency. Further advancements include 4D-Occ [16], which formulates the task as geometric oc-cupancy prediction, and ViDAR [65], which leverages historical images to predict future LiDAR scenes. De-spite these developments, existing prediction-based frame-works generally require multiple historical frames as in-put, still struggle with long-horizon LiDAR forecasting, and provide limited controllability for dynamic scene gen-eration. LiDARCrafter [22] addressed long-horizon gen-eration through an autoregressive diffusion framework, but relies on ego-motion-based extrapolation, a strong prior that restricts the diversity of generated sequences. More recently, U4D [62] introduced uncertainty-aware 4D Li-DAR generation by allocating generation capacity to chal-lenging regions and improving spatio-temporal coherence. In contrast, CrossLiDAR focuses on controllable multi-sensor LiDAR generation, combining long-horizon tempo-ral consistency with calibration-aware cross-sensor align-ment through a ControlNet-based conditioning branch.

3.3. Rectified Flow for LiDAR Generation 

After VAE pretraining, we freeze its encoder/decoder, and model the empirical latent distribution induced by the frozen encoder using a flow-based generative model built on a Transformer backbone, MM-DiT [9]. The Trans-former processes spatio-temporal latent inputs via special-ized temporal-attention and inter-sensor-attention blocks. 

Latent Rectified-Flow Formulation. Given the multi-sensor range image sequence X and Eζ as the pretrained VAE encoder, for the data sample we compute the latent 

z1 = Eζ (X ) ∼ p1(z), and draw an independent noise la-tent z0 ∼ p0(z) = N (0 , I ). For t ∈ [0 , 1] , we form the rectified (linear) bridge zt = (1 − t)z0 + tz 1. Following [9], the timestep t is sampled according to a Logit-Normal dis-tribution to prioritize the mid-range of the transport path. The instantaneous ground-truth velocity is constant along the bridge: 

ddt zt = z1 − z0.

Training. We train a time-dependent vector field vθ (zt, t, c )

(parameterized by the Transformer) to regress this displace-ment by minimizing the rectified flow loss: 

LRF = Et,z 0,z 1 ∥vθ (zt, t, c ) − (z1 − z0)∥22 ,

where c denotes conditioning information (sensor iden-tifiers, textual prompts, bounding-box embeddings, etc.). Under this objective, the optimal vector field is the condi-tional expectation: 

v⋆(zt, t ) = E [z1 − z0 | zt] ,

which provides the probabilistic interpretation of the regres-sion target. 

Inference. At inference, we generate latents by integrating the learned ODE: 

dz dt = vθ (z, t, c ),

from t = 0 to t = 1 . Because the rectified flow objec-tive encourages near-linear transport paths, accurate sam-ples are typically obtainable with a small number of inte-gration steps using a numerical solver (e.g., Euler or mid-point method). Once the latent z(1) is obtained, it is passed through the frozen VAE decoder Dη to reconstruct the multi-sensor range image sequence ˆX . While the model op-erates on range-image latents, the generated range images are back-projected into 3D points using the correspond-ing LiDAR sensor geometry. The reconstructed points are then transformed into the ego-vehicle coordinate frame us-ing calibrated sensor extrinsics and concatenated to form a unified multi-LiDAR point cloud (Appendix 8.1). 

3.4. The Architecture Modules 

MM-DiT Pre-Attention Stage. The generative backbone is implemented as a stack of MM-DiT blocks operating on VAE-compressed latents within the rectified-flow frame-work. Each block processes the latent tokens zt along with auxiliary concatenated modality tokens Φ = {ϕ1(m1) ∥· · · ∥ ϕD (mD )}. Following the canonical MM-DiT design, each block begins with a pre-attention stage utilizing Adap-tive Layer Normalization (AdaLN). The tokens are then processed through a spatio-temporal attention, where 3D ro-tary positional embeddings (3D RoPE) [48] are applied to queries and keys. A dedicated inter-sensor attention is in-serted at this stage to facilitate information exchange across multiple sensors at the same timestep.

LiDAR ControlNet. To enable precise BEV-based struc-tural control over the synthesis process, we incorporate a ControlNet-style branch [70] conditioned on BEV maps containing projected 3D bounding boxes (Appendix 7). Following [70], we create a trainable copy of the MM-DiT backbone blocks while keeping the original generative weights frozen. The ControlNet branch adopts an internal structure iden-tical to the primary modules, including the factorized atten-tion schemes of the LiDAR Sequence and Inter-sensor mod-ules. For each block in this branch, we first fuse the struc-tural control signals (3D bounding boxes and BEV maps), with the current noisy latent zt. Specifically, the control signals are processed by a lightweight encoder to produce the embedding Φctrl , which is summed with zt to form a unified input for the ControlNet blocks. Therefore, the con-trol signals are directly grounded in the current denoising state, allowing for more accurate structural guidance. The output of the n-th ControlNet block, Block (n)ctrl integrates into the corresponding backbone, Block (l)backbone , via a zero-initialized residual connection. 

3.5. Autoregressive Generation 

Na¨ ıve autoregressive LiDAR generation iteratively condi-tions on a model’s own predictions, leading to exposure bias and compounding errors over long horizons [6, 15, 53]. Small inaccuracies in early frames are amplified during roll-out, causing drift and degraded generation quality. To ame-liorate this, we add a small amount of noise to the ground-truth conditioning frame during training. More precisely, rather than using clean conditioning frames during training we fix the noise level of initial frames to t ∈ [0 .7, 1] , encour-aging robustness to exposure bias during autoregression. 

## 4. Experimental Results 

4.1. Experimental Settings 

Datasets. The nuScenes dataset [4] is designed for multi-modal perception in complex urban environments. We uti-lize 32-beam LiDAR sweeps. Each LiDAR sweep (sam-ple) is initially represented as a range image with a reso-lution of 32 × 1024 pixels (totaling 32,768 pixels), a ver-tical Field of View (FOV) spanning (+10 ◦, −30 ◦), and a depth range between 1 and 45 meters. The Waymo Open Dataset [49] utilizes a sensor suite consisting of five Li-DAR sensors. This configuration includes a primary high-performance mid-range LiDAR (Top) for long-range 360 ◦

coverage, supplemented by four short-range side LiDARs (Front, Left, Right, and Rear) positioned around the vehi-cle’s perimeter. These side sensors are specifically designed to capture the immediate surroundings with high precision, providing a unique perspective that is less susceptible to the top-down occlusions common in roof-only configurations. Further details on both datasets are provided in Appendix 6. 

Baselines. We compare CrossLiDAR against a comprehen-sive set of baselines categorized by their underlying repre-sentation. Range-based methods include LiDARGen [74], R2DM [35], RangeLDM [13], LiDM [41], and LiDAR-Crafter [22]. Voxel-based methods include the OpenDWM models [36, 46], and we additionally compare against the occupancy-centric UniScene [19]. Details of these methods under our setting are discussed in Appendix 10.

Evaluation Metrics. Following [22], we evaluate the quality of the generated LiDAR data across three com-plementary representations: (1) Range-image fidelity using Fr´ echet Range Distance (FRD) and Maximum Mean Dis-crepancy (MMD); (2) Point-cloud geometry using Fr´ echet Point Distance (FPD) and MMD; and (3) BEV distribu-tion using Jensen–Shannon Divergence (JSD) and MMD. To evaluate temporal dynamics, we follow [22] by measur-ing frame-to-frame transformation accuracy and sequence smoothness using Temporal Transform Consistency Error (TTCE) and Chamfer Temporal Consistency (CTC). We additionally adapt three metrics from the video synthesis literature to the LiDAR domain to provide a more com-prehensive assessment of (4) Temporal coherence : Blind Video Temporal Consistency (BVC) [2], Fr´ echet Video Distance (FVD) [50], and Fr´ echet Video Motion Distance (FVMD) [24]. Finally, we evaluate rollout quality by ana-lyzing the accumulated point-cloud sequences. MMD val-ues are reported in 10 −4 and JSD values in 10 −2. In all tables, the best result is highlighted in bold and the second-best in underline. We evaluate a fixed, calibrated four-LiDAR configuration, using sensor-specific range-image geometry and extrinsic calibration for ego-frame alignment. More details are provided in Appendix 8.

CrossLiDAR further reduces TTCE by 66 .7% and 64 .0% at steps 3 and 4, respectively, over the second-best method, suggesting the proposed flow-matching transformer better preserves physically plausible ego-motion. CrossLiDAR reduces FVMD by 26 .0% while improving BVC by 104 .7% , demonstrating more realistic motion and stronger temporal stability. These results show CrossLiDAR maintains high quality over long horizons, a key capability for scalable autonomy simulation. 

## 5. Conclusions and Limitations 

We present CrossLiDAR, a unified rectified-flow trans-former for controllable multi-sensor LiDAR generation in a compact range-image latent space. It comprises three key components: an Inter-sensor Module that models ge-ometric dependencies across heterogeneous LiDAR suites, a ControlNet-style conditioning branch for fine-grained structural control via BEV priors and 3D bounding boxes, and a noise-augmented autoregressive strategy that miti-gates cumulative drift over long horizons. On Waymo and nuScenes, CrossLiDAR achieves strong performance in single-frame fidelity, temporal consistency, and long-horizon rollout quality, with particularly strong gains in the multi-sensor setting unsupported by previous methods. Current limitations include the lack of quantitative eval-uation of foreground fidelity and bounding-box adherence. Our evaluation focuses on Waymo’s fixed, calibrated four-LiDAR configuration, motivating further study of sensor conditioning, cross-sensor overlap, and adaptability to dif-ferent sensor counts, types, layouts, and calibrations. The LiDAR Sequence and Inter-sensor Modules are evaluated jointly, leaving their individual contributions unquantified. Evaluation against additional recent 4D LiDAR genera-tion methods remains limited. Downstream benefits for 3D detection, semantic segmentation, occupancy predic-tion, and planning remain unevaluated. Future work will ad-dress richer dynamic object modeling and integration with downstream planning and validation pipelines, while fur-ther reducing the sim-to-real gap. References

nuScenes provides a large corpus of annotated 3D bounding boxes for urban driving scenes captured under diverse traffic, lighting, and weather conditions. Training Procedures. CrossLiDAR follows a three-stage latent generative training protocol. 

VAE Pretraining . We first pre-train the VAE using a recon-struction objective on range images, encoding each LiDAR sensor independently into a compact latent representation. After convergence, the VAE weights are frozen. 

Latent Rectified Flow Training . We then train a Recti-fied Flow model to learn the dynamics in the latent space. For sequence modeling, latent representations from all time steps ( T ) and sensors ( S) are stacked along the batch dimen-sion ( B), enabling joint spatio-temporal learning directly in latent space. 

ControlNet Training . Finally, we freeze the base Recti-fied Flow model and train a ControlNet branch [70] for conditional generation. Control blocks are inserted into the backbone at a fixed interval (one every three backbone blocks). The model is conditioned on structural inputs, in-cluding BEV maps with projected 3D bounding boxes, en-abling controllable scene generation while preserving the pretrained generative prior. 

Training Hyper-parameters. The VAE is trained for 50 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a base learning rate of 4.5 × 10 −6 and a batch size of 16. We employ an 

ℓ1 reconstruction objective with a KL regularization weight of 1 × 10 −6. The encoder—decoder architecture uses a la-tent dimensionality of 4 channels, a base channel width of 64 with multipliers [1 , 2, 2, 4] , and two residual blocks per resolution level with SiLU activations. Circular padding is adopted to respect the periodic structure of range im-ages, and no attention layers are used in the VAE. Mixed-precision (FP16) training is used throughout. The rectified flow model is trained with an ℓ2 loss and a log-normal noise schedule for 500000 iterations using a batch size of 16 . We use AdamW optimizer with learning rate 1×10 −4 for nuScenes, 8×10 −5 for Waymo, β1 = 0 .9,

β2 = 0 .999 , ϵ = 10 −8. The learning rate is linearly warmed up over the first 0.01% of steps and then held constant. Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of 0.1. Mixed-precision (FP16) training is used throughout, with FSDP and zero3 sharding for memory efficiency. During diffusion and ControlNet training, we set the MM-DiT patch size to 1. Multiple conditioning modali-ties are used, including text, images, frame pose, and BEV projections of 3D bounding boxes for explicit scene-level guidance. To enhance robustness and generalization, condi-tion dropout is applied independently to each condition with probability 0.15 ; for short clips (fewer than 5 frames), all conditions are jointly dropped with probability 0.15 . BEV maps are embedded into the VAE latent space using a spatial encoder consisting of 5 layers of 2D convolutional blocks with residual connections. For ControlNet, 30% of the dataset is set aside for train-ing, as reusing the diffusion model data for this stage leads to minimal improvements. 

Sampling and Inference. For generation, we employ rec-tified flow with a linear transport map for the diffusion pro-cess and use the Euler–Maruyama solver for sampling. We run 30 sampling steps with a classifier-free guidance scale of 3.0. Additional sampling hyperparameters are set as needed.

## 9. Ablation Studies 

We conduct ablations on the VAE design choice, the diffu-sion backbone, and other key designs for their validation. Unless specified, all ablation experiments are conducted on the Waymo Open Dataset [49], for multi-sensor LiDAR scene generation ( t = 1 ) setting. 

9.1. VAE 

Our framework adopts the Latent Diffusion [43] paradigm, employing a VAE for compressing high-dimensional range images into a compact latent space. We base our VAE de-sign on recent state-of-the-art architectures, LiDM [41] and RangeLDM [13], and systematically study the impact of key modeling choices. All ablations are performed with a fixed compression ra-tio f = 64 , a batch size of 16, and 50 training epochs, fol-lowing the default settings for each method’s official imple-mentation. Following [19, 36], we adopt an image-based VAE (2D) instead of a temporal 3D architecture, as the goal here is efficient per-frame compression of range im-ages rather than joint spatiotemporal modeling. This archi-tecture also enables more efficient training and inference, similar to designs in [19, 36]. Table 11 presents quantitative VAE reconstruction re-sults for different configurations on Waymo SIDE LiDAR. We observe the following: (1) The use of Circular Con-volution [45] in RangeLDM leads to improved latent rep-resentations relative to LiDM across all metrics (cf. rows 3–6 vs 1–2), confirming the benefit of circularity for sen-sors with 360 ◦ coverage. (2) Incorporating adversarial loss into VAE training is challenging , with performance highly sensitive to the activation schedule; improper tuning can significantly degrade results (cf. rows 1–2 and 3–4). (3) VAE performance deteriorates with aggressive input resiz-ing, likely due to increased information loss (cf. rows 3–4 vs rows 5–6). Nevertheless, this resizing may be necessary for 

compatibility with U-Net-based diffusion backbones, which constrain input resolution and highlight a limitation of the U-Net design. (4) For SIDE LiDAR on Waymo (range 0–20 m), log compression (Equation (17)) provides little benefit, since the dynamic range is limited and linear normalization (Equation (3)) already preserves the range scale adequately without introducing an additional nonlinear transformation (cf. rows 6–7 and Figure 9). 

9.2. Diffusion Backbone: DiT vs U-Net 

For LiDAR generation in the Latent Diffusion frame-work [43], the choice of diffusion backbone is criti-cal. Range images, representing 360 ◦ LiDAR sweeps as anisotropic 2D panoramas, exhibit a pronounced width–height asymmetry: the width spans the full angu-lar resolution, whereas the limited number of sensor beams constrains the height. This results in unique long-range hor-izontal dependencies that differ fundamentally from those in natural images. In this section, we ablate U-Net and Dif-fusion Transformer (DiT) [38] backbones, comparing their effectiveness in modeling these dependencies and their pa-rameter efficiency. While U-Net architectures remain a popular backbone that preserves local structures, they are inherently limited in modeling the extreme aspect ratios and horizontal con-text of range images. In contrast, DiT leverages global self-attention to capture long-range dependencies directly and offers flexible input resolution via dynamic padding, there-fore avoiding the destructive resizing often required by the U-Net. As observed in Section 9.1 and Figure 10, maintain-ing native resolution is crucial , as resizing artifacts can sig-nificantly degrade downstream point cloud reconstruction. Table 12 reports single-frame LiDAR generation results for state-of-the-art methods LiDM [41] and RangeLDM [13] across multiple backbones. LiDM demon-strates strong performance on Waymo SIDE LiDAR gener-ation, notably outperforming RangeLDM (cf. rows 1 vs. 3), with further gains when employing DiT. We find that the default hyperparameters of RangeLDM require more tun-ing (e.g., learning rates), particularly for side LiDARs with smaller dynamic ranges and higher cross-sensor correla-tion demands. DiT-based models outperform U-Nets across most settings (cf. rows 1 vs. 2 and 3 vs. 4), while using approximately 50% fewer parameters and reducing GPU memory consumption to about two-thirds of that required by U-Net. These results highlight the parameter-efficiency advantages of DiT over U-Nets, validating the benefits of transformer-based backbones for LiDAR range image gen-eration. Finally, we perform two ablation studies on the proposed CrossLiDAR architecture. First, we compare DiT and MM-DiT under the same Naive Stacking strategy, where tempo-ral and multi-sensor latent representations are simply con-catenated before being processed by the backbone. This isolates the contribution of the backbone architecture itself. Second, we compare this Naive Stacking baseline against the proposed LiDAR Sequence and Inter-sensor modules while keeping the MM-DiT backbone fixed, therefore iso-lating the contribution of explicit temporal and cross-sensor modeling. Table 12 shows that replacing DiT with MM-DiT under the same Naive Stacking setting improves the results by an average of 10 .1% across all evaluation met-rics. In contrast, replacing Naive Stacking with the pro-posed LiDAR Sequence and Inter-sensor modules yields substantially larger gains, reducing FRD by 88 .8% , point-cloud FPD by 90 .9% , and BEV JSD by 50 .0% relative to the MM-DiT baseline. These results demonstrate that the improvements reported in the main paper are largely driven by the combined proposed temporal and cross-sensor mod-ules rather than by the MM-DiT backbone alone.

10.4. OpenDWM 

Adaptation for Waymo . We adapt the LiDAR generation components provided in the OpenDWM codebase 8 to the Waymo SIDE LiDAR setting. Specifically, we use its Li-DAR VAE tokenizer together with the temporal DiT diffu-sion model. Because the released configuration is designed for sequence generation from a single LIDAR TOP stream, we replace the original single-sensor setup with the four SIDE sensors (front, left, rear, right) and apply the same naive multi-sensor stacking strategy used for LiDM and RangeLDM (Figure 11). Each sensor is encoded indepen-dently using a shared VAE. The resulting latent tensors are then stacked along the sensor and temporal dimensions be-fore being processed by the temporal diffusion transformer. We otherwise retain the point-cloud voxelization and latent parameterization from the official release, which voxelizes the scene within a [−50 , 50] × [−50 , 50] × [−3, 5] m vol-ume with voxel sizes (0 .15625 , 0.15625 , 0.125) and uses a 32-channel latent representation.

## 11. Waymo Qualitative Results 

In this section, we present qualitative results on the Waymo dataset. We highlight the impact of our architectural mod-ules on the generation quality, demonstrating improvements in cross-sensor alignment, temporal consistency, and con-trollable scene synthesis. 

Inter-sensor Alignment. The Inter-sensor Module pro-vides clear benefits by enabling joint modeling of all Li-DAR sensors within the same temporal frame. Instead of processing each sensor independently, the module al-lows the network to learn direct interactions across view-points through attention. As shown in Figure 12, the mod-ule enables coherent multi-view integration, leading to im-proved alignment and more accurate scene understanding compared to the independent sensor generation of the com-petitors. 

Temporal Consistency. One of the key capabilities of our approach, enabled by the LiDAR Sequence Module , is the modeling of long-range spatio-temporal dependencies within each sensor stream. By jointly attending over spa-tial tokens across multiple time steps under a causal con-straint, the module captures motion patterns and temporal context while preventing information leakage from future frames. This design enforces temporal coherence during generation and improves reasoning over evolving 3D struc-tures. As shown in Figure 13, the LiDAR Sequence Mod-ule yields smoother predictions, more stable object repre-sentations over time, and enhanced dynamic scene under-standing. 

Controllable LiDAR Generation. By conditioning the model on 3D bounding boxes and BEV maps through the 

ControlNet-style branch, we enable precise object-level control during generation. These structural cues guide the generation, allowing the model to accurately place objects according to the specified layout. As shown in Figure 13, this mechanism reliably regulates object presence and spa-tial arrangement, resulting in strong alignment between the input structural constraints and the generated LiDAR scenes.
