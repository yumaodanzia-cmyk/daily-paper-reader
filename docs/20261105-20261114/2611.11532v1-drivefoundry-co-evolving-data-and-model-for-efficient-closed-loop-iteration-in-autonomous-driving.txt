Abstract
A driving foundation model must learn reusable decision knowledge from rare critical events and
apply it under strict runtime constraints. We introduce DriveFoundry, a closed-loop framework
designed to improve iteration efficiency under data and computation budgets by coupling structured
causal data refinement with adaptive inference. The Data Engine builds traceable scene–candidate
records that link scene evidence and factor–decision relations to candidate trajectories and outcome
assessments. Candidate comparisons and counterfactual annotations clarify how scene factors
affect behavioral feasibility and preference. Verified model failures guide VLM-assisted annotation
and expert correction, refining both the training data and the annotation system for subsequent
learning.
The model engine, Xiaomi-Drive-o1, unifies Perception, Causation, Action, World,
and Value within a shared vision–language backbone, enabling cross-task knowledge transfer
through joint training. Block-wise latent refill passes candidate-specific evidence and predicted
visual consequences between task blocks, connecting scene understanding, planning, and outcome
evaluation. A learned value-of-computation head selects additional tasks and allocates latent
tokens according to their expected benefit and incremental cost, adapting inference to the demands
of each decision.
On PhysicalAI, the 9B model achieves the lowest ADE@1 across both evaluated splits and horizons
among the tested models. In AlpaSim, it outperforms Alpamayo 1.5 on all reported metrics and
achieves lower close-encounter and off-road rates than the 34B Alpamayo 2 Super, but trails it
in progress and AlpaSim scores. For the 0.8B model, joint training reduces 5 s minADE@6 by
approximately 54% without changing the inference path or token budget. Adaptive inference mode
further balances the performance of full reasoning while using 28% of its tokens. Real-vehicle tests
additionally demonstrate runtime feasibility. These results support a unified approach to learning
from critical experience and adapting computation to the demands of each driving decision.

causal annotation, Multitask learning, Adaptive computation.

Introduction
Progress in physical intelligence hinges on converting physical experience into generalizable behavior under
acceptable budgets [9, 17, 19]. This is particularly acute in production-level autonomous driving (SAE
L2/L3) [13], where vehicles act in a partially observed world and their decisions shape subsequent interac-
tions [80]. Each control cycle therefore requires identifying decision-relevant factors from observation histories,
generating executable motion plans, predicting their consequences and selecting among alternatives under
constraints. Fleet expert clips offer broad coverage of normal driving, yet failures that expose the system
limits are condensed in critical events [15, 45, 48]. Improving in this regime requires a costly cycle of discovery
and learning. The central learning objective for autonomous driving should therefore be closed-loop iteration
efficiency for critical scenarios [33], learning transferable decision relationships from rare experiences at an
acceptable cost and reusing them in subsequent driving.

General-purpose vision-language models (VLMs) provide visual and semantic representations [28, 70, 71, 87],
but driving expertise further requires precise geometric and motion modeling, decision-relevant reasoning,
candidate comparison and real-time control. This motivates unified learning across perception, decision and
value, beyond direct observation–action mapping. Driving VLMs and vision-language-action models (VLAs)
extend supervision to scene understanding, explanations and action prediction [26, 41, 59, 66, 73, 79, 89], while
vision-action models (VAs) learn direct mappings through multitask pretraining [32, 49, 78]. Explicit language
reasoning captures scene relationships and decision rationales, but introduces information bottlenecks and
sequential decoding costs. World-action models (WAMs) predict action-conditioned futures [36, 65, 77, 92, 94],
yet prediction alone does not explain which factors justify an action or favor it over counterfactual alternatives.
Latent reasoning offers compact task representations [40, 51, 57], but fixed-capacity, one-step interfaces
constrain adaptation to scene demands. These developments motivate shared latent reasoning that links scene
evidence, action choices and outcome evaluation, with adaptive computation for efficient closed-loop iteration
3


under limited data and compute budgets.
For a data engine, closed-loop iteration efficiency depends on coverage of critical experience and supervision
density, supporting compositional generalization and reusable causal abstraction [3]. Data flywheels improve
coverage and label quality through failure mining, targeted annotation and continual learning [5, 16, 91],
while generative world models expand experience through controllable synthesis and action-conditioned
prediction [1, 18, 23, 74]. World Engine integrates these capabilities with behavior modeling and reinforcement
post-training, expanding rare critical scenarios for policy improvement [39]. Still, rollouts alone do not
identify which evidence, causal relationships or behaviors require correction. Structured language annotations,
including graph-structured QA, Chain of Causation (CoC) and structured chains of thought [60, 66, 69, 82,
86], complement experience expansion by linking grounded evidence, interactions, decision rationales and
outcomes. Effective abstraction further requires supervision of how causal factors determine the suitability
and consequences of alternative behaviors. A data engine should therefore connect evidence, candidate
decisions and consequences through structured causal annotations, using failure diagnosis and feedback to
refine supervision so that corrections transfer across cases.
We introduce DriveFoundry (Figure 1), a framework for learning reusable driving behavior from critical
experience under constrained data and computation budgets. Its common interface is a scene–candidate
record linking scene facts, a candidate’s semantic intent and continuous trajectory, and the evidence used to
assess its outcome. Multiple candidates expose alternatives to the recorded behavior, while partial records
allow annotation effort to focus on the distinctions that matter to the decision.
The model engine, Xiaomi-Drive-o1, connects Perception, Causation, Action, World, and Value tasks through
a shared vision–language backbone. Shared parameters support learning across tasks and scenes; block-wise
latent refill passes intermediate results within the current decision. Inference starts with a fast Action–Value
path. A value-of-computation (VOC) head guides further computation to expand candidate coverage or
improve candidate evaluation when the expected benefit justifies the additional cost.
The two feedback processes operate at different timescales. Online inference allocates computation to the
current decision. Offline iteration traces failures to gaps in evidence, candidate coverage, or outcome assessment,
then revises the corresponding supervision. Shared scene and candidate identifiers connect these revisions to
both the driving model and the annotation system.
Our contributions are threefold:
• A closed-loop framework and structured causal data engine. We introduce DriveFoundry, which
connects data refinement, model learning, and adaptive inference under data and computation budgets.
Its scene–candidate records combine temporal factor–decision relations, candidate comparisons, and
counterfactual assessments into reusable supervision. Verified failures guide targeted annotation and
subsequent updates to both the driving model and the annotation system.
• A unified driving foundation model with latent refill. We develop Xiaomi-Drive-o1, integrating Perception,
Causation, Action, World, and Value through a shared vision–language backbone. Shared parameters
allow cross-task learning to strengthen fast Action–Value inference, while block-wise latent refill connects
intermediate results and preserves candidate correspondence across planning, future prediction, and
evaluation. Staged training connects semantic and visual pretraining to continuous planning, with task
supervision retained during reinforcement post-training.
• Learned allocation of inference computation. A dedicated value-of-computation head estimates the
benefit of further reasoning after accounting for its incremental execution cost. It learns from SFT
rollouts using candidate-evaluation supervision shared with Value. At inference, it uses the already
computed state to guide additional task execution and latent-token allocations under compute, latency,
and memory constraints.

3.1
Overview
DriveFoundry links data annotation, model learning and inference to improve closed-loop iteration efficiency.
Its common unit is a scene–candidate record connecting scene evidence, candidate behaviors and outcome
assessments. These records provide complementary supervision for a shared driving model, while failures
guide subsequent annotation and training updates. The two loops operate at different timescales: offline
iteration improves the learned capabilities and online inference allocates computation to the current decision.
Data annotation loop.
The Data Engine organizes experience into scene facts and relations, semantic
decisions and trajectories, and behavioral assessments. Multiple candidates expose alternatives to the recorded
behavior, while partial records focus annotation on the information needed to resolve a failure. VLM-assisted
annotation, human correction and verification produce training data and improve the annotation system itself.
Model cognition loop.
Xiaomi-Drive-o1 connects Perception, Causation, Action, World, and Value through a
shared vision–language backbone, detailed in Figure 2. Shared parameters support learning across tasks and
scenes. Latent refill passes intermediate information within a decision. The scheduler selects additional tasks
and their latent-token budgets when further computation is useful. The following sections describe these
model interfaces, their supervision, and the training and feedback processes that connect the two loops.

3.5
Inference Scheduling
Inference scheduling adapts the task path and latent-token budgets to the needs of the current decision,
as illustrated in Figure 8. Each decision begins with the Action–Value path: Action generates candidate
trajectories, Value predicts their problem probabilities across the five evaluation dimensions, and Check applies
rule-based feasibility tests. Rule-based rewards and learned Value assessments jointly rank the admissible
candidates. If the selected candidate meets the acceptance criteria, inference terminates without invoking
additional tasks. When the initial result is insufficient, the scheduler uses the trained VOC head to compare
feasible computation expansions. These expansions can activate Perception, Causation, or World, or increase
the latent-token budgets of these tasks; the Action latent-token budget remains fixed. Value assesses the
current candidates, whereas VOC estimates how much an expansion could improve the final choice relative to
stopping, after accounting for its additional cost. The scheduler combines these estimates with candidate
acceptance criteria and the remaining resources to decide whether and how to continue computation.
Each selected expansion follows the task dependencies. Perception and Causation provide additional scene
evidence and factor–decision relations for Action to generate updated candidates. World instead conditions
on a generated candidate and predicts its visual consequences, supplying future evidence for Value. Latent
refill carries the realized intermediate outputs to the downstream blocks, keeping candidate generation and
evaluation aligned with the information actually computed. A skipped block contributes no new result, and
any downstream recomputation required by an expansion is included in its incremental cost.
After an expansion, the updated candidates are re-evaluated, problematic candidates are filtered, and the
remaining candidates are re-ranked. The highest-ranked candidate is returned if it meets the acceptance
criteria; otherwise, the system generates further candidates or expands reasoning while resources permit.
When no acceptable candidate is available and the remaining budget cannot support further computation, the
predefined fallback procedure is invoked. Because online rule rewards can miss problematic Action proposals,
on-vehicle validation checks the candidates and their predicted consequences to intercept identifiable problems
before execution.

separately. The scheduler admits additional calls only when time and memory remain available, including the
reserve for control execution.
3.6
Closed-Loop Data and Model Iteration
Offline iteration uses driving failures to identify gaps in supervision. Diagnosis examines the generated
candidates, their supporting evidence, and their evaluation. Missing behaviors motivate additional decision
or trajectory targets. Incorrect candidate rankings motivate review of consequence and Value assessments.
Feasibility errors motivate review of scene facts, factor relations, and checks.
Incorrect rule judgments, problems missed by Value, and World prediction errors identified during training or
deployment are fed back into the data loop. Human review identifies which scene–candidate fields require
correction and updates the annotation model, labeled candidate records, or rules accordingly. Newly generated
candidates are included when constructing DV for subsequent training phases. Evaluation of the updated
model checks improvement on related cases and supplies the next set of failures.

4
Experiments
The evaluation focuses on two questions: whether structured causal supervision improves driving understanding
and planning, and whether Value-guided dynamic reasoning allocates computation effectively. Using Qwen3.5-
VL as the pretrained vision–language backbone, we compare Xiaomi-Drive-o1 with Alpamayo 1.5 [60] Alpamayo
2 Super [30] and Qwen-Drive-1.0 [99] on NVIDIA’s PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles [61] dataset using a
shared evaluation protocol that combines semantic causal assessment with geometric planning assessment.
4.1
Evaluation Protocol and Metrics
We evaluate Xiaomi-Drive-o1 on NVIDIA’s PhysicalAI–Autonomous–Vehicles (PAI-AV) benchmark [61]. The
benchmark provides the scenes and recorded trajectories used to construct the linked annotation records
in Section B; each evaluation unit therefore retains the correspondence among a scene, candidate behavior,
expected consequence, and value label. Our high-quality test subset is drawn from clips excluded from our
19


training and validation data. Candidate-selection rules and inference thresholds are calibrated on the validation
split. Comparisons include Alpamayo 1.5 (AR1.5) [60], Alpamayo 2 Super (AR2) [30], and Qwen-Drive-1.0 [99].
For every run, we record the released checkpoint, input configuration, output format, candidate count, and
inference budget. The protocol scores observable outputs under a common scorer and does not assume that
an AR baseline exposes the dedicated Perception, World, or Value heads used by Xiaomi-Drive-o1.
Open-loop PhysicalAI evaluation.
PAI-AV requires each method to predict six candidate trajectories for
every scene. We evaluate the selected trajectory with ADE@1 and the candidate set with minADE@6, where
ADE@1 measures the geometric accuracy of the directly executed prediction and minADE@6 measures whether
at least one candidate covers the recorded motion. Both metrics are reported at 3 s and 5 s horizons, with lower
values indicating better trajectory fidelity. The standard 644-example Physical-AI NV split overlaps publicly
available training data by its official construction. To make the comparison leakage-aware, we additionally
report results on a high-quality subset curated exclusively from held-out test clips;
Closed-loop AlpaSim evaluation.
AlpaSim evaluates behavior after the ego vehicle interacts with the
simulated scene, exposing errors that accumulate beyond the recorded trajectory. We run 1,607 scenarios
with PAI-AV-NuRec version 26.04. Following the evaluation protocol used by Alpamayo [82], we report
close-encounter rate for all and at-fault events, off-road rate, progress, and AlpaSim score for all and at-fault
events. Lower values are preferred for encounter and off-road rates, whereas higher values are preferred for
progress and score. The at-fault variants exclude events not caused by the ego vehicle; together, these metrics
measure close-proximity events, roadway departures, scenario progress, and distance traveled between events.

Adversarial causal disambiguation.
Table 12 isolates explicit causal reasoning with two 200-clip scenarios
under a 1:1 mixture of visually similar positive–negative pairs, with examples shown in Figure 12. The
comparison tests whether the causal chain can identify lane-topology intrusion and the cause of a stationary
obstacle before action selection, using decision F1 and clip pass rate as complementary measures.
• Causal disambiguation. Given only four frames over two seconds, the A + P baseline often confuses a
roadside barrier with lane-blocking roadwork or queued traffic with a disabled vehicle, yielding decision
F1 scores of 68.5% on Scenario A and 57.0% on Scenario B. The P →C →A path uses Causation
latents to represent whether an obstacle intrudes into the drivable lane and why it is stationary, then
passes this information to Action through latent refill. Decision F1 rises to 89.0% and 82.5% (+20.5 and

45.5% to 73.5% on Scenario B.
• Early detour commitment. The CoC-conditioned path supports earlier detour commitment and helps
avoid near-obstacle failures. The baseline often waits until the ego vehicle is close to a cone or stopped
vehicle before changing lanes; on visually similar positive–negative pairs, this late response produces
unstable decisions. In stationary-vehicle detours, the delayed maneuver can leave the end of the 6-second
open-loop evaluation trajectory in a safety-risk region, so clip pass rate trails decision F1. By identifying
the obstacle’s effect on lane feasibility earlier, CoC enables a timely maneuver.

5
Conclusion
We introduced DriveFoundry, a framework that connects learning from critical driving experience with
adaptive computation at decision time. Its Data Engine organizes scene evidence, factor–decision relations,
candidate trajectories, and outcome assessments into traceable scene–candidate records. Its model engine,
Xiaomi-Drive-o1, integrates Perception, Causation, Action, World, and Value through a shared vision–language
backbone. Shared weights transfer learning across tasks and support fast-path inference; candidate-conditioned
latent refill carries intermediate results between tasks; and a dedicated value-of-computation head guides
further reasoning. Verified failures feed back into both annotation and driving-model updates, connecting
inference-time decisions with subsequent learning. Public-benchmark comparisons and targeted ablations
demonstrate strong trajectory prediction and closed-loop driving performance, with benefits from additional
task modules, joint training, and adaptive inference. Real-vehicle tests further demonstrate on-vehicle runtime
feasibility. These results support the integration of scene understanding, candidate generation, consequence
prediction, and evaluation within a shared driving model.
The broader contribution is an integrated technical foundation for Xiaomi spanning data production, model
learning, adaptive inference, and vehicle validation. Its central principle is to make critical experience reusable:
failures can be examined in terms of missing evidence, omitted candidate behaviors, or inaccurate outcome
assessments, allowing verified corrections to inform subsequent annotation and training. This establishes a
concrete path toward learning more transferable decision knowledge from each critical event and directing
additional computation to decisions where it is expected to improve the final choice.
26


Limitations.
Structured causal supervision depends on scene coverage and annotation quality. The CoC
and counterfactual evaluations measure agreement with reviewed labels; controlled changes to scene factors
are still needed to assess whether the learned relationships support appropriate closed-loop behavior under
unfamiliar conditions. Learning from this supervision also depends on the balance among task objectives.
The gradient-alignment analysis describes a local condition that can vary across tasks, scenes, and training
stages, leaving the trade-off between planning improvement and auxiliary-task retention to be evaluated over
repeated training cycles.
At inference, candidate selection is limited by both behavioral coverage and evaluation reliability. World
receives visual supervision at 1, 2, and 3 s, whereas Action plans over 6.4 s, leaving longer-horizon consequences
without direct World supervision. Shared representations may also correlate World and Value errors, while
verification cannot recover unproposed behaviors or correct every scene-interpretation error. These limitations
also affect computation allocation.
Adaptive inference operates over supported task and latent-budget
configurations, and its VOC estimates, learned from offline expansions of an SFT checkpoint, may become
inaccurate as the model, scene distribution, or execution costs change. Detached scheduling inputs do not
remove this shift, so scheduling records and allocation quality require reassessment across checkpoints and
deployment platforms.

Appendix
A
Related Work
A.1
VLM and VLA for Autonomous Driving
Vision-language models (VLMs) connect visual observations with language supervision for driving-scene
understanding, building on visual instruction tuning and general-purpose multimodal language models [2,
46, 47, 75]. LingoQA [59] evaluates video-based driving question answering, while DriveLM [66] organizes
perception, prediction, and planning questions through graph-structured dependencies. OmniDrive [79]
extends this supervision to holistic scene understanding and counterfactual reasoning. DriveGPT4 [89]
further connects visual evidence and language explanations with end-to-end vehicle control. Driving-specific
benchmarks [4, 8, 22, 53, 62] provide complementary coverage of VQA, map understanding, and scene-level
assessment. Together, these efforts establish language as an interface for expressing scene semantics and
relating them to driving behavior.
Vision-language-action (VLA) models incorporate action prediction into the multimodal interface, connecting
semantic reasoning with executable motion. General VLA systems [35, 102] transfer visual-language knowledge
into control and open-ended action prediction, while driving planners [10, 12, 25, 43, 98, 103] have progressed
from sensor-fusion and vectorized planning to trajectory-generating models. Recent driving VLAs add
cognitive, causal, or world-model signals: Alpamayo-R1 [82] couples structured Chain-of-Causation (CoC)
traces with trajectory generation, FutureSightDrive [95] introduces spatio-temporal reasoning for future-aware
planning, and related systems [6, 21, 27, 41, 50, 55, 56, 72, 100] combine multimodal reasoning with end-to-end
action learning. World-model-based driving methods [23, 24, 81, 83] provide future-state supervision or
interactive prediction. These approaches motivate treating scene understanding, reasoning, and action as
interdependent capabilities. DriveFoundry extends this organization to include auxiliary perception, causal
decision formation, action-conditioned future prediction, and candidate evaluation within a shared latent
interface. Its supervision connects discrete policy decisions to continuous trajectories and their evaluated
consequences, giving these capabilities a common scene–candidate reference.
A.2
Chain-of-Thought Reasoning: Explicit and Latent Forms
Explicit Chain-of-Thought. Explicit Chain-of-Thought (CoT) [14, 37, 52, 84] represents intermediate reasoning
as a sequence of discrete language tokens between an observation and a final prediction. Its central benefit is
supervision: intermediate steps expose which evidence supports a conclusion and can improve compositional
reasoning. Grounded visual explanations [34] connect such steps to visual entities and spatial relations. For
autonomous driving, an effective trace should identify relevant agents and traffic constraints, reason about
their interactions and likely consequences, and use this evidence to select a maneuver. Such supervision
teaches how a decision is formed, rather than only how its final trajectory is decoded.
Driving-oriented models increasingly instantiate this structure. DriveLMM-o1 [29] learns step-by-step driving
traces; FutureSightDrive [95] introduces spatio-temporal reasoning to connect current observations with future
outcomes; and Alpamayo-R1 [82] organizes the trace as a structured Chain-of-Causation (CoC) that conditions
trajectory generation. CoC gives this textual interface a driving-specific structure by linking decision-relevant
evidence to the maneuver being explained or predicted. It therefore makes the evidence–decision relationship
explicit while retaining the inspectable form of language supervision.
The same discrete representation creates a systems trade-off for closed-loop driving. Autoregressive decoding
incurs computation for every generated token, so latency grows with trace length [51, 54]. Language tokens
describe constraints semantically but do not directly encode the continuous geometry and dynamics required for
a feasible trajectory. These limitations motivate latent and implicit CoT methods, which retain intermediate
computation in continuous hidden states and are discussed next.

Driving-specific methods connect latent computation to spatial, temporal, and action prediction. LaST-

organization of latent computation around decision dependencies. Perception, CoC, Action, World, and Value
concepts have distinct supervision targets, and block-wise autoregressive refill makes an upstream block’s
output available to downstream queries. This allows evidence extraction, candidate formation, consequence
prediction, and evaluation to share intermediate states while retaining their different roles in the decision
process.
A.3
Adaptive Reasoning and Computation Allocation
Adaptive reasoning makes inference computation depend on the scene and the expected benefit of further
reasoning. AdaThinkDrive [54] studies how explicit CoT affects driving across different scenario complexities
and learns to select between direct prediction and reasoning-conditioned prediction. AutoVLA [100], LaST-
R1 [6], and cognitive or adaptive world-model planners [42, 76] extend this idea through reinforcement
fine-tuning, physical latent reasoning, or adaptive future prediction. Their hybrid supervised and reinforcement-
learning objectives jointly account for trajectory quality and reasoning economy. These methods establish
a concrete alternative to executing the same reasoning procedure for every input: learn when intermediate
reasoning improves the action enough to justify its computational cost.
Efficient VLM/VLA systems [7, 51, 96, 97] also study token routing, block-wise execution, and reduced-cost
action decoding. In parallel, closed-loop reinforcement-learning and policy-refinement methods [31, 45, 63, 67,
90] optimize action quality under safety or interaction feedback. Our method connects this allocation decision
to candidate verification. An initial Action–Value computation produces and evaluates a candidate. An initial
Action–Value computation generates and evaluates candidates. The VOC head then guides the selection of
task execution and latent-token allocations for Perception, Causation, and World, using the current inference
state and remaining budget. This adapts both task activation and the amount of latent computation within
each enabled task. The shared latent state carries information across these routes, while Value feedback links
additional computation to the quality of the current candidate. Adaptive inference thus operates over the
same evidence, decision, and consequence structure used by the annotation system.
B
Unified Causal Data Annotation Loop
B.1
Motivation, Overall Principles, and Annotation-Model Iteration
The development efficiency of a production autonomous-driving system depends not only on open-loop or
closed-loop performance on fixed evaluation sets, but also on how efficiently real-vehicle risks can be converted
into learnable data and then into improved driving experience. Conventional data systems often center on
a single type of supervision, such as trajectories, object boxes, or lane lines. Such supervision supports
perception and behavioral imitation, but it does not fully specify which scene facts affect a decision, why a
candidate behavior is selected, or how the outcomes of different candidates differ.
Annotation contents differ substantially in information complexity, production cost, throughput, and degree
of automation. Object geometry, road elements, and basic states can be generated at scale by rules, sensor-
geometry systems, and autolabeling models. Driving relations, decision factors, causal explanations, and
value judgments require more expensive model assistance or human annotation. DriveFoundry therefore uses
hierarchical annotation both to organize information and to allocate data-production resources: low-cost,
high-throughput supervision is used mainly for pretraining, while high-cost, high-value supervision is used for
Chain-of-Causation (CoC) reasoning, planning SFT, and Value Model training.
36


DriveFoundry is designed to support multiple tasks and training stages. Structured annotations provide
a common factual source from which Perception QA, CoC QA, decision and causal SFT data, planning
trajectory data, and Value evaluation data are derived according to task requirements. World learns to predict
visual observations at future horizons of 1, 2, and 3 s, conditioned on the scene history and a candidate
trajectory. Its visual targets are derived from image sequences and linked to the corresponding scene and
candidate records.
The system follows four principles. First, supervision is stratified by content and production cost. Second,
automatic annotation provides scale, while human annotation supplies complex relations, causal explanations,
decisions, and value judgments. Third, low-cost Scene annotation covers basic scene facts, while the evidence
required by a concrete driving candidate, decision segment, or Critical Factor in Policy determines where
expensive annotation and review are allocated. Fourth, human annotation, annotation models, and quality
review form a loop that progressively increases annotation automation.
Annotation automation follows an iterative process. Human annotators first build high-quality seed data,
which is used to train annotation models for different label types. The models then pre-label new data in
batches, while humans focus on low-confidence samples, long-tail scenes, and cases in which models disagree.
Reviewed results are added back to the training set and used to update the annotation models and rules. The
process applies to objects, road relations, decisions, Critical Factors, and CoC. For CoC, humans initially
identify Critical Factor instances, bind the associated decision labels to those factors, and annotate their
attributes and roles to establish reliable supervision. As the annotation model improves, production gradually
shifts toward model-based labeling, human sampling inspection, and difficult-case review.
B.2
Unified Causation Annotation System
The basic data unit of DriveFoundry is a scene–candidate record. Every record is based on recorded driving
data that includes the actual ego trajectory. The scene describes environmental observations and ego state
over the time interval, while the candidate in the base record denotes the executed driving behavior, including
discrete decisions and a continuous trajectory. When counterfactual analysis is required, additional candidate
behaviors can be introduced under the same scene and planning horizon and linked to their feasibility, outcome
judgments, and value evaluations. Each record preserves label sources, confidence, and evidence references,
and distinguishes observed outcomes from counterfactual outcome judgments. The annotation system groups
labels into Scene, Policy, and Value. Scene labels describe observed entities, states, and relations; Policy labels
describe factor–decision relations and behavior; Value labels assess individual candidates. These annotation
families supply supervision for the model’s Perception, Causation, Action, and Value tasks. Each supervision
family is stratified by content complexity, production method, and training use. Table 13 summarizes the
hierarchy and its downstream uses.
Scene annotation is organized progressively from physical evidence to structured state and then to scene
relations. L0 Physical / Evidence records physical evidence that is directly observed or computed. L1
Structured State organizes objects, road elements, signals, and ego state into a structured state. L2 Driving
Relation describes the objective relations among these entities.
Policy annotation uses the continuous driving decision process as its organizing object and expands across
physical execution, decision segments, decision instances, and counterfactual candidates. The Physical /
Execution level records actual ego motion, action execution, the recorded trajectory, velocity, acceleration,
and spatial relations, while referencing the corresponding Scene states. The Event level represents Decision
Segments obtained by partitioning an entire clip through rule–model collaboration. Rules generate lateral and
longitudinal action keyframes defined by Meta Action, while human annotators mark lateral and longitudinal
semantic keyframes. Rules and large language models then jointly merge and filter these keyframes to produce
event-level temporal segments, termed Decision Segments. The Decision level consists of factor-decision
causal pairs within each Decision Segment. Annotation first identifies a Critical Factor instance and then
binds the associated lateral or longitudinal decision label to that factor. The resulting pair constitutes a
Decision Instance. A segment may contain multiple instances, which retain their own valid time ranges and
are managed asynchronously across different factors and lateral or longitudinal decisions. The Counterfactual
level records controlled changes to scene factors and comparisons among alternative candidate behaviors.

B.3
Deriving Task Data from Structured Annotations
Structured annotations provide a common factual source for Perception, CoC, Action, and Value tasks.
Perception pretraining uses hierarchical QA translated from Scene states L0–L2 labels and covers physical
evidence, structured state, and objective scene relations. CoC pretraining jointly uses Scene and Policy
ground truth to generate QA about scene evidence, Decision Segments, Critical Factor instance extraction,
decision-label binding on each factor, and the attributes and roles of the resulting factor-decision causal pairs
(Decision Instances). Because Scene and Policy are hierarchical, the derived QA retains the source level,
question type, and difficulty. Value pretraining also uses textual analyses of candidate trajectories in the
five evaluation dimensions. The continuous Action decoder is introduced during SFT. Figure 15(c) maps the
structured annotations to task data and separates the raw-image World-model interface.
A large language model performs linguistic translation during QA production but does not create facts. Every
QA example must trace back to its source record, source fields, and annotation version. After generation,
factual-consistency checks prevent the QA from introducing objects, states, relations, or future information
that are absent from the structured ground truth.
During SFT, Perception learns from the corresponding scene labels, while Causation learns factor extraction,
decision-label binding, relation roles, and grounded CoC explanations. Action learns meta-actions and
continuous trajectories. Temporal annotations align the factors and decisions with the corresponding trajectory
segments.
World predicts visual tokens for future observations at 1, 2, and 3 s, conditioned on the scene history and
a specific candidate trajectory. For recorded behavior, the corresponding future observations provide the
supervision targets. Alternative candidates require their own valid future targets; the recorded future is
not automatically assigned to them. World loss is computed only on executed World paths with valid
candidate-matched targets.
Value uses the five-dimensional binary problem labels and continuous quality supervision defined above, with
invalid or inapplicable targets masked. These signals are retained separately during data production.
For post-training, human-refined assessments of recorded trajectories train and calibrate the annotation
model. The calibrated annotator evaluates diverse candidates generated by the SFT model, forming fixed
scene–candidate supervision for Value.
VOC supervision reuses these candidate assessments. Alternative task and latent-budget expansions are
executed from the same saved SFT inference prefix, recording the current state, proposed task and latent-
budget configuration, selected candidate, and incremental execution cost. Value learns individual candidate
quality; VOC learns the improvement in the selected candidate relative to stopping, minus the additional
execution cost.
These rollout annotations are prepared offline before post-training to reduce generation and annotation
overhead. Online collection is also possible. Rule rewards for Action are computed separately during RL.

B.5
Decision-Centric CoC Annotation Process
A frame-level CoC record can pair a Meta Action keyframe with one factor and one decision label to explain
the recorded trajectory. Restricting annotation to this form leaves six aspects of continuous decision-making
under-specified:
1. No temporal representation of the decision process. A frame-level Decision Label and Critical Factor
cannot describe the process from intent formation through execution, completion, or interruption, nor
can they represent changes in the entry, exit, and influence of associated factors.
2. No asynchronous lateral and longitudinal decisions. Lateral and longitudinal decisions have different
formation, persistence, and switching rhythms, which are difficult to express with a frame-level label or
a joint lateral–longitudinal category.
3. No representation of multiple objects and simultaneous decisions. A vehicle may turn, yield to one
object, and pass before another object at the same time. A single decision label loses the factors and
composition of these local decisions.
4. No planning-role semantics for Critical Factors. The same scene factor may motivate or maintain
a decision, or constrain the feasible set for realizing it. Merely recording a Critical Factor does not
distinguish these roles.
5. No counterfactual candidate analysis. Recording only the executed trajectory prevents comparison
with alternative trajectories in safety, efficiency, comfort, and rule compliance, and cannot distinguish
an infeasible candidate from a feasible but inferior one.
6. Unstable machine semantics in free-text CoC. Free text can introduce hallucination and post-hoc
rationalization and is difficult to validate as a closed set, analyze statistically, or use as stable ground
truth.
DriveFoundry represents CoC as a temporally structured set of factor-decision causal pairs grounded in Scene
evidence. This representation supports concurrent lateral and longitudinal decisions and traceable causal
explanations across a driving clip.
Meta Action defines the lateral and longitudinal actions used by rules to generate action keyframes over the
clip. Human annotators separately mark lateral and longitudinal semantic keyframes reflecting meaningful
decision boundaries. Rules and large language models jointly merge and filter the two keyframe sources,
retaining the temporal anchors used to partition the clip into Decision Segments with explicit start and end
times. These segments constitute the Event level, as shown in Figure 17(a).
Within each Decision Segment, annotation first extracts Critical Factor instances and forms a Critical Factor
List. It then binds the associated lateral or longitudinal decision label to each factor and records the applicable
role or roles (MOTIVE, CONSTRAINT, or both), valid time range, source object, source state, affected aspect,
and evidence source. Each resulting factor-decision causal pair constitutes a Decision Instance at the Decision
level; the decision label is the decision end of the pair rather than a separate instance. These instances are
managed asynchronously according to their factors, decision dimensions, and valid time ranges, allowing
multiple local decisions to coexist. Figure 17(b) summarizes this factor extraction and decision-label binding.
Changes in factor state, role, or bound label are recorded on the corresponding pair timeline, with object
identity, state, and relation references kept consistent with Scene evidence.
Structured CoC organizes the Decision Instances within each segment, together with their temporal relations
and outcomes. These explicitly typed relations can be checked for consistency. Natural-language reasoning
derives a concise causal explanation from these relations for CoC SFT, analysis, and debugging. Each
explanation states the relevant factors before the decisions they support, using the explicit form “Because
[factors], the ego vehicle [decisions].” The clauses trace back to the same structured pairs and evidence
available at the decision time. Trajectory alignment uses the actual ego trajectory already recorded in the
driving data and associates it with the valid time ranges of the Decision Segment, Decision Instance, and
Critical Factors. It describes how a decision is realized as action execution and whether the action is completed,
interrupted, or switched.
42


Counterfactual annotation covers both changes to scene factors and comparisons among alternative behaviors.
Factor interventions specify a physically consistent change and assess its effect on decision feasibility or
candidate preference.
For behavior comparisons, at least two candidates with alternative semantic decisions or trajectories are
evaluated under the same scene history and planning horizon. Feasibility and mandatory constraints are
checked first. Feasible candidates are then compared using the five-dimensional assessments and continuous
quality measures. Each judgment retains its evidence source and confidence, including those for unexecuted
candidates.

B.6
Quality Control and Annotation-Model Iteration Loop
Before release, the data undergo stratified quality checks. Rule labels are tested with unit tests and boundary-
case regression. Autolabeling results are sampled by category and scenario and checked for cross-frame
consistency. The fusion module is checked for model–rule conflict rate, topological validity, and temporal
consistency. CoC annotation is checked for action and semantic keyframe coverage, rule–LLM merging and
filtering, Event-level decision-segment boundaries, membership of each factor-decision causal pair as one
Decision Instance within its segment, and asynchronous intervals across factors and lateral or longitudinal
labels. Critical Factor instance extraction is checked for omission, wrong selection, duplicates, and object-
identity consistency; attribute and role annotation is checked for accurate binding of each lateral or longitudinal
decision label to its factor, agreement between the causal-pair record and Decision Instance identity and
valid time range, state accuracy, MOTIVE/CONSTRAINT and affected-aspect consistency, valid time ranges,
and Scene evidence references. Decision causes must be supported by evidence available at the decision
time, while observed outcomes and decision evidence are recorded separately. Value annotation is checked
for consistency in candidate comparison, the rate of incomparable cases, and conflicts among dimensions.
QA data are checked for source-field traceability and factual correctness. Figure 18 connects these checks to
accepted-data release, issue-specific corrections, and annotation-model iteration. Its quality gate also checks
the temporal CoC example in Figure 5: each explanation must include all factors valid in its interval, preserve
the associated decisions, and place the factor clause before the decision clause.
Quality control is also the entry point for annotation-model iteration. Human-confirmed high-quality labels
43


are added to the corresponding annotation-model training set. The model pre-labels new data in batches,
and confidence, scene difficulty, and model disagreement determine which samples require human review.
Low-quality rules trigger rule revision; weak model performance on particular scenes triggers difficult-sample
mining and retraining; disagreement among human annotators triggers specification updates and expert
adjudication; and factual errors in QA trigger updates to generation templates or translation models.
For CoC, human-confirmed Critical Factor instances, their bound decision labels, factor attributes and roles,
and Structured CoC continuously train and update the annotation model. Evaluation covers factor-instance
extraction accuracy and completeness, factor–label binding accuracy, consistency of pair and instance identity
and timing, and the consistency of role semantics and evidence references. Weak scenes are fed into the
next round of sampling and human review. As performance improves, human work shifts toward high-value
samples, low-confidence review, and long-tail evaluation, forming a loop of seed annotation, batch model
pre-labeling, difficult-case review, and model retraining.
Verified failures from development evaluation and fleet operation also guide scene selection, candidate
generation, and targeted label correction. Reviewed records feed subsequent driving-model training as well as
annotation-model updates.

To qualitatively evaluate our model’s ability to understand driving scenes, we first compare the CoC outputs of
our method and AR2 on six cases (Fig. 19). We then present 60 representative visualized cases (Figs. 20–29).
For each scene, we list the critical factors that the ego vehicle must attend to, the CoC, the lateral and
longitudinal decisions the ego vehicle should take, and the counterfactual QA pairs.
Figure 30 presents qualitative results of our world model on the Physical AI dataset. We examine future-
frame generation under both ground-truth trajectory conditioning and counterfactual interventions. The
counterfactual examples modify the conditioning trajectory through mirroring or speed scaling, illustrating
how the generated visual predictions respond to changes in the specified ego motion. Figure 31 presents
four qualitative examples of planning results from Xiaomi-Drive-o1 and the corresponding CoC boxes on the
PhysicalAI test set.
