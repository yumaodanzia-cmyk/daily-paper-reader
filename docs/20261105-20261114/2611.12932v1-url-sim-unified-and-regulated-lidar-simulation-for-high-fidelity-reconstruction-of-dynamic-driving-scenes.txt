URL-Sim: Unified and Regulated LiDAR Simulation for
High-Fidelity Reconstruction of Dynamic Driving Scenes
Kefei Qian, Xipeng Feng, Jie Li, Long Cao, Yuhan Chen, Xiangfei Huang, Guofa Li
Abstract—Reconstructing
high-fidelity
LiDAR
simulation
scenes from real-world driving logs is essential for cost-effective
safety validation of autonomous driving systems. While simula-
tors based on Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) achieve realistic
scene reconstruction, their large-scale adoption is limited by
slow rendering speeds. Recent 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS)
approaches have improved rendering efficiency through explicit
primitive representation and differentiable rasterization. How-
ever, existing 3DGS-based methods remain largely restricted
to rigid vehicles and produce redundant primitives through
unconstrained densification. To address these limitations, we
propose URL-Sim, a unified and regulated dynamic LiDAR
simulation framework. URL-Sim constructs a dynamic Gaussian
scene graph that decouples the modeling of traffic participants,
including vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists, enabling accurate
reconstruction of heterogeneous dynamic objects within a single
framework. It further introduces a regulated Gaussian densifica-
tion strategy that ranks primitives with multi-dimensional impor-
tance scores and allocates Gaussians to the most critical regions
while keeping the total primitive count bounded. Experiments
on the Waymo and KITTI-360 datasets demonstrate that URL-
Sim achieves higher quantitative accuracy and visual quality
than existing baselines under comparable primitive budgets, and
accurately recovers the geometry and poses of diverse dynamic
traffic participants.
Index Terms—LiDAR simulation, Gaussian splatting, dynamic
scene graph, non-rigid modeling, autonomous driving.

and realistic LiDAR simulation methods [3].
Traditional LiDAR simulation, whether physics-engine-
based virtual worlds [4], [5], [6] or explicit geometric re-
construction [7], [8], [9], [10], suffers from substantial sim-
to-real gaps, cumbersome pipelines, and limited support for
dynamic scenes. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [11] narrow
the sim-to-real gap for LiDAR simulation using continuous im-
plicit representations and differentiable volume rendering [12],
[13], [14], [15]. Some methods incorporate spatio-temporal

The consequent slow training and rendering speeds cannot

Recent 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) [18] has emerged as
an effective alternative to NeRF, achieving order-of-magnitude
improvements in rendering efficiency through its explicit
Gaussian representation and hardware-accelerated rendering.
While current mainstream methods [19], [20], [21], [22]
primarily focus on camera data synthesis, seminal works such
as GS-LiDAR [23] and LiDAR-GS [24] have successfully
adapted 3DGS to the LiDAR imaging model by designing
differentiable splatting pipelines tailored to LiDAR’s active
perception mechanism. To handle dynamic scenes, LiDAR-
RT [25] and SplatAD [26] employ a dynamic-static sepa-
ration strategy to model rigid vehicles separately. Despite
these advances, existing Gaussian-based LiDAR simulation
methods face two primary challenges. First, current dynamic
scene simulations primarily focus on rigid objects and provide
limited support for non-rigid entities such as pedestrians and
cyclists, who are vulnerable road users central to high-risk
edge cases. Second, existing methods incur substantial com-
putational overhead, driven by the inherent sparsity and non-
uniform angular distribution of LiDAR point clouds, combined
with the unrestricted densification of the Gaussian paradigm.
These issues reduce training efficiency and simulation perfor-
mance, potentially triggering training failures and constraining
practical scalability and scene adaptability.
To address these challenges, URL-Sim is proposed as a uni-
fied and regulated LiDAR simulation framework for the high-
fidelity reconstruction and controllable editing of dynamic
urban driving scenes. URL-Sim constructs a dynamic Gaussian
scene graph that decouples individual dynamic entities from
the static background for independent modeling. Rigid objects
such as vehicles are simulated via standard rigid transforma-
tions. For non-rigid entities such as pedestrians, the Skinned
Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) [27] model is introduced to serve
as a deformation prior. Specifically, the Gaussian primitives of
each pedestrian are bound to the SMPL mesh vertices, and the
pose and shape parameters of the model are utilized to drive
the skinning deformation of the Gaussians. For template-free
non-rigid objects such as cyclists, a lightweight deformation
network is employed to directly learn and optimize the local
deformation parameters. Together, these three representations
enable unified modeling and simulation of the principal traffic
participants in autonomous driving.

II. RELATED WORK
A. LiDAR Simulation and Novel View Synthesis
Classical LiDAR simulation relies on virtual platforms such
as CARLA [4] and AirSim [5] for environmental control and
closed-loop interaction [6], but inherently suffers from severe
sim-to-real domain gaps. Efforts to bridge this gap through
physical modeling [7] and real-world asset libraries [8], [9]
are hindered by cumbersome procedures and high labor costs.
Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [11] have advanced Li-
DAR simulation through implicit spatial modeling and volume
rendering, narrowing the sim-to-real gap. Early frameworks
including NFL [12] and LiDAR-NeRF [13] integrate the Li-
DAR imaging process into NeRF to enable high-quality novel
view synthesis. For complex dynamic scenes, UniSim [14]
and NeuRAD [15] utilize feature grids and precise physical
modeling to reconstruct and recompose traffic participants.
DyNFL [17] and LiDAR4D [16] further isolate dynamic
objects and capture temporal correlations to achieve editable
digital twins and high-fidelity 4D spatio-temporal synthesis.
Nevertheless, the reliance on high-frequency dense sampling
induces considerable computational overhead and slow render-
ing speeds, precluding real-time closed-loop deployment.
3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) [18] offers a real-time alter-
native via its explicit primitive representation and highly paral-
lelized rasterization. Early 3DGS-based methods such as GS-
LiDAR [23] and LiDAR-GS [24] render substantially faster
than NeRF-based counterparts with comparable reconstruction
quality, but are limited to static scenes and lack editability.
LiDAR-RT [25] separates dynamic and static components
using bounding boxes and tracking IDs to enable customizable
scene editing. SplatAD [26] and SIMULI [28] similarly rely
on 3DGS for joint camera-LiDAR simulation. However, these
approaches are predominantly restricted to rigid vehicles and
overlook non-rigid objects such as pedestrians and cyclists.
Moreover, current frameworks lack resource-efficient mech-
anisms for controlling redundant Gaussians resulting from
unconstrained densification.
B. Gaussian Resource Optimization
Although 3DGS demonstrates promising sensor simulation
capabilities, its heuristic cloning and splitting generate redun-
dant Gaussian primitives, inflating VRAM consumption and
storage [29], [30]. Various approaches have been proposed to
optimize the computational efficiency of 3DGS for large-scale
deployment.
The first category targets post-processing of redundant
models via compression and pruning. Compact 3DGS [31]
and Compressed 3DGS [32] employ vector quantization and
entropy coding to compress Gaussian parameters, reducing
storage without altering underlying spatial geometry. Prun-
ing strategies eliminate Gaussian primitives with marginal
visual contributions, guided by metrics such as multi-view
consistency, uncertainty scores, and global importance [33],
[34], [35]. Although model volume is substantially reduced,
excessive pruning at extreme compression ratios frequently
leads to severe visual degradation and loss of foreground
geometric details.
The second category optimizes densification during train-
ing to prevent redundant generation at its source, replacing
unconstrained growth with rigorous control. SteepGS [29]
and Taming 3DGS [36] minimize redundant sub-Gaussians
through analytical optimization and fixed-budget construc-
tive pipelines. BUGS [37] employs a region-consistency-
based bi-directional growing mechanism to adaptively regu-
late Gaussian density for compact scene reconstruction. For
LiDAR geometry, GTLR-GS [38] and GeomGS [39] incorpo-
rate curvature-adaptive refinement and geometric confidence
scores to govern densification. These works demonstrate that
proactive densification is critical for compact, high-fidelity
reconstruction.
Nevertheless, these strategies are designed for visual modal-
ities and rely on dense pixel-level observations. When ap-
plied directly to LiDAR simulation, they may misallocate
Gaussian primitives by overlooking sparse ray-level geometry
and intensity cues, thereby compromising range accuracy and
foreground structural fidelity.

A. Dynamic Gaussian Scene Graph
URL-Sim decomposes the raw LiDAR scene into a static
background model Gbg and various dynamic instance models,
comprising rigid objects Grg, SMPL-based humans Gsmpl,
and other non-rigid deformable entities Gdfm. Each model
is parameterized by a distinct set of Gaussian primitives,
each of which independently learns its specific geometric and
reflectance properties. To represent scene geometry more ac-
curately, the 2D Gaussian splatting (2DGS)[40] is selected as
the basic element, which shares a common set of parameters:
G = {µ, S, R, o, ζ, β}
(1)
Specifically, each Gaussian primitive is parameterized by a
mean position µ, scaling factor S, rotation quaternion R,
opacity o, intensity ζ, and ray drop probability β, all of
which are learnable. The variables S and R jointly define
the covariance matrix as Σ = RSS⊤R⊤. Notably, ζ ∈R1
and β ∈[0, 1] are LiDAR-specific physical attributes repre-
senting the reflectance and no-return characteristics of real-
world laser beams, respectively. Following the formulation
in LiDAR-RT [25], the ray drop probability β is derived
via the Softmax normalization of two logit parameters, βdrop
and βhit. Since LiDAR intensity and ray penetration behavior
strongly depend on the viewing direction, spherical harmonic
(SH) coefficients are employed to explicitly model the joint
parameters (ζ, βdrop, βhit) in an anisotropic manner.
Dynamic objects are identified via tracked bounding boxes
and masked to isolate the static background. The remain-
ing point clouds are aggregated across frames and voxel-
downsampled to initialize the static model Gbg.

B. Regulated Gaussian Densification
The Adaptive Density Control (ADC) in 3DGS faces a crit-
ical dilemma in LiDAR simulation: its unconstrained growth
leads to massive primitive proliferation and VRAM overflow,
whereas enforcing a hard capacity limit causes premature
saturation that halts densification and degrades reconstruction
quality.
To address these limitations, a regulated Gaussian densifica-
tion (RGD) strategy is proposed. A maximum growth budget
is allocated for each step based on user-defined constraints
to ensure a strictly predictable densification process. The
primitive count scales with the number of densification steps
according to a quadratic growth trend [36]:
Q(x) = B −(B −S)(1 −x/N)2
(6)
where B denotes the final user-defined maximum number
of Gaussians (the budget), S represents the initialized total
number of Gaussians, and N indicates the total number of
densification rounds.
To guide Gaussian densification and pruning within this
budgeted framework, we develop a comprehensive importance
scoring mechanism. Beyond simple gradient-based heuristics,
we compute a saliency score for each primitive by integrating
temporal reconstruction errors, geometric attributes, visibility,
and edge responses. Consequently, model capacity is prefer-
entially allocated to regions exhibiting structural complexity,
high visibility, and challenging reconstruction conditions.

and high-error regions, an edge-aware importance map Mt ∈

structed from two components. The first component is a pixel-

rendered and ground-truth depth and intensity maps. The sec-

LiDAR ground-truth. Departing from conventional visual edge

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
URL-Sim is validated on the widely used Waymo Open
Dataset [42] and KITTI-360 [43]. The Waymo data were
collected at 10 Hz using a 64-beam LiDAR with a 20° vertical
field of view (FOV), natively providing depth and intensity
images at a resolution of 64×2560. KITTI-360 employs a 64-
beam LiDAR operating at 10 Hz with a 26.4° vertical FOV.
The raw KITTI-360 LiDAR data are spherically projected
into 2D depth and intensity images at a 64×1030 resolution.
Four dynamic scene sequences containing non-rigid dynamic
instances were selected from each dataset. For each sequence,
50 consecutive frames were extracted, with every tenth frame
allocated to the test set and the remainder used for training.
Comprehensive model evaluation relies on a quantitative
framework that targets two primary dimensions: 2D perceptual
quality and 3D geometric accuracy. For 2D projections, pixel-
level depth and intensity deviations are measured via Root
Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Median Absolute Error
(MedAE), with global fidelity assessed through SSIM [44],
Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and LPIPS [45]. In 3D
space, structural discrepancy is measured using Chamfer Dis-
tance (CD) [46] and F-Score at a 5 cm threshold. Rendering
efficiency is evaluated via frame rate (FPS) and total Gaussian

C. Comparison with the SOTAs
URL-Sim is evaluated against state-of-the-art (SOTA)
NeRF- and 3DGS-based frameworks, including LiDAR-NeRF
[13], DyNFL [17], LiDAR4D [16], LiDAR-GS [24], GS-
LiDAR [23], and LiDAR-RT [25]. All baselines were imple-
mented using their official open-source codebases and assessed
using a unified set of quantitative metrics. Consistent pseudo-
color mapping schemes are applied for visualizing depth and
intensity maps. All experiments were run on a single NVIDIA
RTX 5070 Ti GPU for 30,000 iterations.
Tables IV and V summarize the quantitative results on
the Waymo and KITTI-360 datasets. All 3DGS-based base-
lines are capped at a budget of approximately 3 million
Gaussians for fair comparison. URL-Sim achieves compet-
itive rendering speeds while processing non-rigid dynamic
instances. It achieves the best performance on most 2D image
rendering metrics with substantial improvements in intensity
and depth PSNR. These gains are driven by effective non-
rigid dynamic modeling and selective optimization of high-
contribution Gaussians guided by RGD. Qualitative compar-
isons in Fig. 5 demonstrate that previous methods struggle with
geometric smearing and ghosting when handling non-rigid
objects. URL-Sim recovers clear depth profiles and structural
intensity details for non-rigid objects. The resulting recon-
structions exhibit substantially sharper geometric boundaries
that are consistent with the ground-truth spatial structure and
physical intensity distributions.
URL-Sim also achieves the highest 3D geometric accuracy,
with the lowest Chamfer Distance and highest F-score on
both datasets. Compared to the second-best baseline LiDAR-

V. CONCLUSION
This paper presents URL-Sim, a unified and regulated
LiDAR simulation framework that builds a dynamic Gaussian
scene graph for decoupled modeling and high-fidelity recon-
struction of heterogeneous traffic participants. Integrating the
SMPL prior with a lightweight deformation network bridges
the gap in non-rigid LiDAR simulation, while the proposed
RGD strategy employs multi-dimensional importance scoring
and fixed budget allocation to direct resources toward critical
regions under a fixed primitive budget. Extensive experiments
on Waymo and KITTI-360 demonstrate significant improve-
ments over existing baselines in both perceptual quality and
geometric accuracy.
Although URL-Sim delivers competitive dynamic LiDAR
simulation, certain limitations remain. First, the dynamic de-
coupling relies on accurate bounding boxes and tracking, and
reconstruction quality may degrade in crowded environments.
Additionally, large viewpoint shifts during editing may distort
unobserved regions. Integrating generative priors such as dif-
fusion models represents a promising direction for overcoming
these extrapolation challenges.
