Abstract— Rough terrain traversability is often estimated
through point-wise roll, pitch, or force-angle margins along
a planned path. However, these metrics are often inaccurate
and overly conservative because they ignore how terrain
disturbances and low-level controller corrections can affect
the vehicle’s motions. To overcome these limitations, we in-
troduce Execution-Grounded Traversability (EGT), defined as
the probability that a vehicle–controller pair can execute a
candidate path while meeting trajectory-level tracking and
progress requirements. We estimate EGT by predicting the
distribution of closed-loop trajectories using a latent-space flow
matching (FM) model conditioned on multi-resolution terrain
features and 3D vehicle attitude and wheel-ground contact
points prior. Across 650 simulated trials on unseen terrains,
EGT reduces safe-path rejection to 2.2% (versus 11.4%–39.5%
for pose-margin and force-angle baselines) and unsafe accep-
tance to 7.7%. Integrated into a receding-horizon sampling-
based planner, EGT reduces navigation failure rates by 24–42
and 30–44 percentage points over [1] and [2], respectively, while
cutting goal-reaching time by up to 20.5% and 45%. Finally, we
demonstrate zero-shot transfer of our simulation-trained model
to real-world outdoor experiments.
I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous wheeled vehicles used outdoors for applica-
tions such as search and rescue, construction, and forestry
must accurately evaluate whether they can follow a reference
path over rough terrain without tipping over or getting stuck.
Standard traversability methods evaluate safety along this
path using tip-over stability margins [3] or limits on terrain-
induced roll and pitch angles [4], [5]. However, relying on
attitude limits or tip-over margins to evaluate traversability—
even dynamic metrics like the force-angle measure [3]—
tends to be both overly conservative and inaccurate for three
key reasons:

1) Pointwise limits ignore closed-loop recovery (overly
conservative): Checking stability or attitude limits at ev-
ery point along a path penalizes brief posture excursions.
A large transient roll or pitch angle does not make a
path infeasible if the vehicle’s momentum and low-level
controller can ensure a safe recovery.
2) Rotational stability does not guarantee traversal (in-
accurate): Force-angle and attitude limits only check
for rotational tip-over. Even when a vehicle stays within
safe posture limits and tip-over margins, wheel slip can
stop forward progress or push the vehicle sideways into
obstacles or ditches.
3) Reference-path checks ignore closed-loop deviation
(inaccurate): Calculating posture or tip-over margins
along the reference path may provide limited insight
because terrain disturbances and subsequent controller
corrections can make the vehicle depart substantially from
the planned reference.
Therefore, in this paper, we do not define traversability
from terrain and posture features alone; instead, we evaluate
whether a specific vehicle–controller pair can execute a ref-
erence path under closed-loop control. Formally, we define
Execution-Grounded Traversability (EGT) as the probability
that a vehicle–controller pair can execute a candidate refer-
ence path while keeping the path-tracking error below a set
limit and completing a required fraction of its arc length. This
execution-based perspective reshapes how we factor vehicle
stability into traversability assessments. Specifically, our core
insight is that physical failures—like tipping into a ditch or
stalling on a steep slope—directly derail path tracking or halt
forward progress. Conversely, satisfying attitude or force-
angle bounds alone does not guarantee that the vehicle will

complete the path.
To compute EGT, we must predict the distribution of
closed-loop path-tracking behavior on rough terrain. One
approach, proposed by Wang et al. [6], learns an open-
loop, action-conditioned dynamics model and pairs it with
a tracking controller at inference time. In effect, the learned
model acts as a simulator, generating closed-loop executions
through repeated evaluations of the dynamics model and a
low-level controller. However, this recursive rollout com-
pounds prediction errors: an inaccurate state prediction alters
the controller’s corrective action, which further degrades sub-
sequent predictions. Moreover, these corrections can push the
model toward state–action pairs that are poorly represented in
the training data, further reducing accuracy. Finally, and most
importantly, their learned target is action-conditioned vehicle
dynamics, rather than the complete execution distribution
conditioned on a reference path.
In this paper, we bypass the recursive rollouts of [6]
by treating the vehicle and its feedback controller as a
single closed-loop system. Rather than simulating step-by-
step dynamics, we directly learn the distribution of realized
trajectories from closed-loop executions in simulation. We
model this conditional distribution using a latent-space flow
matching (FM) architecture conditioned on multi-resolution
terrain features and a 3D kinematic pose–contact prior. Given
this context and a candidate path, our learned FM model
jointly predicts the entire sequence of closed-loop waypoints,
without recursive evaluations of vehicle dynamics and a
tracking controller.
Our EGT method provides two main advantages. First,
across 650 simulated trials on unseen terrains, EGT reaches
97.4% classification accuracy. It is much less conservative
and more accurate than the force-angle measure [3] and
roll/pitch limit methods [4], [5], cutting the conservative
rejection of safe paths from 11.4%–39.5% down to 2.2% and
unsafe acceptance down to 7.7%. Second, when we use EGT
with conditional value-at-risk (CVaR) execution costs in a
simple receding-horizon sampling-based planner, we reduce
failure rates by 24–42 percentage points over [1] and 30–44
percentage points over [2]. At the same time, our planner
reduces mean goal-reaching time by up to 20.5% and 45%
over these baselines.
In summary, the contributions of our work are:
• We introduce EGT, a probabilistic traversability metric
based on closed-loop execution requirements.
• We develop a closed-loop trajectory predictor based on
latent FM that allows us to estimate EGT along a reference
path.
• We combine EGT with risk-aware planning and demon-
strate improvements in path classification and navigation.
II. RELATED WORK
This section extends the comparisons with existing work
presented in Section I.
A. Motion Prediction and Learned Traversability
Traversability methods can assess predicted vehicle motion
or directly estimate terrain costs. Takemura and Ishigami [4]
combine a quasi-dynamic model with a tracking controller
to predict trajectories. They assess these trajectories using
limits on roll, pitch, and slip. Learned predictive models
also support motion-based traversability assessment [2], [7].
Wang et al. [6] combine learned probabilistic dynamics
with a tracking controller to predict closed-loop executions
through recursive model–controller evaluations.
Other methods map terrain observations directly to
traversability costs or scores. Castro et al. [8] learn velocity-
conditioned costs from visual and geometric features, using
IMU measurements for supervision. WVN [9] predicts visual
traversability scores using velocity-tracking errors as self-
supervision. RoadRunner [10] predicts traversability maps
from camera and LiDAR measurements, using labels gen-
erated in hindsight. TNT [11] maps terrain elevation to
traversability costs. Its supervision uses vehicle attitude,
differences between commanded and executed motion, and
kinodynamic prediction uncertainty. Other approaches learn
terrain costs from expert demonstrations through inverse
reinforcement learning [12], or directly predict risk-sensitive
costs [13].
Our FM model directly predicts a joint distribution of com-
plete closed-loop trajectories for a reference path and a fixed
vehicle–controller pair. This avoids recursive dynamics–
controller rollouts during prediction. It also supports path-
specific assessment from predicted executions, rather than
from local terrain scores alone.
B. Probabilistic Traversability and Risk-Aware Planning
Probabilistic methods account for uncertainty in traversabil-
ity assessment. Leininger et al. [1] constructed sparse
Gaussian-process-based elevation maps. They used the de-
rived terrain slope, uncertainty, and footprint assessment
as the traversability metric. STEP [14] combines terrain
uncertainty, conditional value-at-risk (CVaR), and model pre-
dictive control. PIETRA [15] predicts uncertainty in traction
and vehicle attitude. It also uses CVaR to assess traction risk
and constrain roll and pitch.
Our approach follows a risk-aware framework similar
to those of the above works. However, EGT provides the
probabilistic traversability estimate directly over closed-loop
executions for a given reference path and controller. The
effects of traction loss and tip-over are captured when they
cause violations of the tracking or progress requirements.

IV. VALIDATION AND BENCHMARKING
This section has two objectives. First, we evaluate how
accurately EGT captures a wheeled vehicle’s ability to tra-
verse a given rough terrain. Second, we assess the impact of
EGT on downstream planning performance by benchmarking
against existing navigation algorithms.
A. Implementation Details
We implemented the FM model, EGT assessment, and
sampling-based planner in JAX, using Equinox [19] as the
training framework. We adapt the method in [20] as our
low-level path-tracking controller. The training data were
collected in ROS Gazebo using a Husky vehicle. Intel
RealSense was used to obtain terrain information in both
simulation and real-world runs. The training dataset consisted
of closed-loop executions of 8,808 feed-forward trajectories
on 7 different terrains.
For all experiments, we use a constant corridor half-width
w(s) = 1.0 m, minimum progress fraction β = 0.8, and
required EGT probability preq = 0.8. Each candidate is
evaluated using ns = 30 predicted executions. At every
replanning cycle, the planner evaluates nc = 100 candidate
reference paths. Both execution-risk terms use a CVaR level
of α⊥= αprog = 0.90, and the objective weights in (20) are
λg = λ⊥= λprog = 0.5.
Real-world implementation was done on a skid-steered
robot Jackal which is similar to the vehicle used in training.
The computations were performed on the mounted RTX 3090
laptop. The planning took an average of 1s, while the low-
level path-tracking controller ran at 20Hz.
The vehicle remains at rest during replanning and begins
tracking the selected reference only after planning is com-
plete. Each new execution therefore starts from rest, consis-
tent with the initial-state assumption of the FM predictor.

C. Quantitative Benchmarking of EGT
EGT classifies a path as traversable when bpegt ≥preq,
with preq = 0.8 and computed from the predictions of the
closed-loop executions through FM. We compare it with two
alternatives:
• Force-Angle Measure [3]: This baseline evaluates tip-
over stability along the reference path using gravitational
loading only. We use [18] to estimate the vehicle’s 3D
pose and wheel–ground contact points. At each evaluated
reference waypoint, we compute the force-angle margins
about all tip-over axes. A path is accepted only if the
minimum margin is strictly greater than zero at every
evaluated waypoint. Inertial loads are not included in this
baseline.
• Pose-Margin: Based on [4], [5], this baseline checks
whether the vehicle’s pitch and roll angles along the refer-
ence path exceed a set threshold. We evaluate thresholds
of 45◦, 60◦, and 75◦to examine the trade-off between
conservative rejection and incorrect acceptance.
Methodology: We evaluate traversability classification over
650 simulated trials. All trials use terrains that were not
included in the FM training data. In each trial, the vehicle–
controller pair executes a reference path once in simulation.
An execution is labeled safe if the vehicle reaches the
goal without tipping over, getting stuck, or exceeding the
prescribed lateral-deviation limit. Otherwise, it is labeled
unsafe. The lateral limit is the same as the tracking tolerance
used in EGT, w(s) = 1.0 m. Here, safe and unsafe refer to
successful and unsuccessful reference-path execution under
these task requirements. All methods are evaluated against
the same outcome labels, which are assigned from the actual
recorded simulation.

executions. Let nr denote the number of safe executions
incorrectly rejected, and na the number of unsafe execu-

executions. These counts give a conservative-rejection rate
of 2.2%, an unsafe-acceptance rate of 7.7%, and an overall
classification accuracy of 97.4%.
Without conditioning on the 3D pose and contact-point
prior from [18], EGT rejects 29 of 598 successful execu-
tions and accepts 37 of 52 unsuccessful executions. The
corresponding error rates increase to 4.8% and 71.2%, while
overall accuracy decreases to 89.8%. This ablation shows
the importance of the pose prior for the evaluated execution
predictor. It should be noted that for the pose-prior abla-
tion, we train a separate FM model without pose–contact
conditioning, using the same training dataset and training
procedure.
Overall, these results provide two key validations. First,
the pose limits and tip-over stability margins measured
through force-angle measure, can reject executable paths
while also often accepting paths that fail the execution
requirements. Second, EGT probability estimated through the
learned FM model reduces both types of error by assessing
predicted closed-loop execution.

V. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
We presented Execution-Grounded Traversability (EGT)
to assess whether a vehicle–controller pair can follow a
rough-terrain path. Using closed-loop trajectories predicted
by a flow-matching model, EGT computes the probability of
satisfying path-tracking and progress constraints. Across 650
simulated trials, EGT achieved 97.4% classification accuracy,
reducing conservative rejection and unsafe acceptance over
3D-pose baselines. The EGT planner achieved 2%–4% fail-
ure rates and the lowest mean goal-reaching times across
three terrains. The real-world validation is presented in Fig. 1
and the accompanying video, which includes experimental
comparison with [1]. Finally, since our planner accounts for
the potential low-level controller’s corrective actions through
FM-based execution predictions, it reduces the reliance on
frequent reference-path re-planning while retaining high-rate
feedback control.
Limitations and Future Work: Our EGT assessment as-
sumes rough, non-deformable terrains. In the future, we
plan to train the FM model on deformable surfaces and
condition it on tokens derived from both point clouds and
images. Furthermore, embeddings from vision foundation
models could be integrated into the FM architecture. In this
work, we also assume a fixed vehicle–controller pair. Thus, a
promising direction is to enable the FM architecture to take
the vehicle embodiment and low-level control structure as
additional context. Finally, EGT evaluates execution along
the reference path, but it does not check stability past the
endpoint. To address this limitation, the planner can replan
before the path ends—for example, halfway through—and
extend the prediction horizon past the previous endpoint.
We are also looking to reduce the computation time of our
planner. A major overhead stems from the computation of
3D kinematic prior which in the current work is obtained
from solving a non-linear least squares (NLS) problem [18].
One way to reduce this overhead is to replace the NLS with
a learned surrogate.
