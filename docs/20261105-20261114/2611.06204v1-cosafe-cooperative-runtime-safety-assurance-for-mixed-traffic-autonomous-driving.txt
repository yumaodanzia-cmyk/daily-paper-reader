# COSAFE : Cooperative Runtime Safety Assurance for Mixed-Traffic Autonomous Driving 

Dingji Wang ∗, You Lu ∗, Haowen Jiang ∗, Yanggang Chen ∗, Yuyang Bi ∗,Bihuan Chen ∗‡ , Shenglin Bao †, Xin Peng ∗∗College of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Fudan University, Shanghai, China

Abstract —With the rapid development of autonomous driving systems (ADSes), real-world roads are evolving into mixed-traffic environments where autonomous vehicles (AVs) and other traffic participants ( e.g., human-driven vehicles (HDVs), cyclists, and pedestrians) coexist and interact with each other. How-ever, current ADSes still incur various safety violations in mixed traffic scenarios, raising a strong demand for runtime safety assurance frameworks that can continuously monitor hazards and prevent potential violations. Existing ego-centric assurance approaches are insufficient for mixed traffic, where violations often arise from locally reasonable yet jointly incompatible decisions among multiple vehicles and cannot be reliably resolved through independent takeover. To bridge this gap, we propose C OSAFE , a modular and ADS-agnostic cooperative runtime safety assurance framework for mixed-traffic autonomous driving to extend runtime safety assur-ance from ego-centric takeover to scene-level cooperative interven-tion. C OSAFE leverages vehicle-to-everything (V2X) communica-tion to enable roadside units (RSUs) to monitor scene-level hazards and jointly mitigate hazards involving multiple vehicles. We integrate C OSAFE with seven state-of-the-art ADSes, and our eval-uation on the InterDrive benchmark demonstrates that C OSAFE 

improves the driving score of AVs by 70.03% on average, reduces safety violations by 87.80%, and accurately identifies unsafe AVs with an F 3 score of 0.930, while satisfying real-time constraints. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

With the rapid development of ADSes, real-world roads are increasingly evolving into mixed-traffic environments where AVs ( i.e., vehicles equipped with ADSes) and other traffic participants ( e.g., HDVs, cyclists, and pedestrians) coexist and interact [54]. However, the performance of AVs in open-road mixed traffic remains far from satisfactory. Scenario-based testing approaches [12, 43, 66] have indicated that even advanced ADSes may still incur safety violations in mixed-traffic environments. Besides, a 2024 U.S. Department of Transportation analysis of California ADS disengagement reports [31] found that 69% of disengagements were caused by ADS insufficiency. These limitations underscore the need for runtime safety assurance frameworks to prevent safety violations in mixed-traffic environments. Runtime safety assurance [53] for ADSes aims to con-tinuously monitor driving hazards, intervene before poten-tial safety violations occur, and maintain safe operation at run-time. To prevent safety violations, several approaches moni-tor basic safety properties and trigger emergency braking [33, 65] or conservative fallback maneuvers [10, 44] before these 💥

However, these approaches rely on predetermined emergency interventions rather than adapting to the specific hazardous scenarios encountered. To intervene more adaptively, REDriver [52] intervenes in the planning process by refining ADS-generated planned trajectories to enforce specification compliance, but it requires intrusive modifications to the original ADS stack, and is tied to Apollo [3]. Without modifying the underlying ADSes, A RGUS [53] formulates runtime assurance as a hazard-aware takeover-and-recovery process for the trajectory of one single AV. However, these ego-centric approaches assess and mitigate hazards on a per-vehicle basis, treating each AV as an inde-pendent takeover target rather than viewing mixed traffic as a software-intensive cyber-physical system of interacting AVs, other traffic participants, and RSUs [46]. As shown in Fig. 1a, two ADS-controlled AVs may generate trajectories that conflict at an unsignalized intersection. Although independently taking over each AV can avoid an immediate collision, the two AVs may enter a circular wait-for chain because neither vehicle has a scene-level view to determine yield priority, causing a deadlock in Fig. 1b. Mixed-traffic safety violations often arise from lo-cally reasonable yet jointly incompatible decisions among mul-tiple vehicles, which cannot be reliably resolved via ego-centric takeover. As shown in Fig. 1c, resolving this interaction-induced safety violation requires scene-level intervention, where one AV is assigned to proceed while the other slows down and yields. Such intervention, which no single AV can reliably produce on its own, requires a scene-level view across multiple vehicles. arXiv:2611.06204v1 [cs.SE] 4 Nov 2026 V2X communication provides a practical foundation for this view, allowing AVs to exchange state, intent, and agreement-seeking information with RSUs. However, most existing V2X-enabled cooperative driving approaches still leave the driving decisions to individual vehicles, which may incur safety vio-lations in mixed traffic. Representative work includes shared perception [15, 37, 42, 55, 62], decentralized multi-vehicle plan-ning [8, 68, 69], and LLM-mediated negotiation [18, 20, 36]. In contrast, centralized multi-vehicle planning approaches [25, 26, 39] coordinate traffic behavior at the scene level, but they typically assume an AV-dominated fleet with homogeneous ADSes under direct centralized control, a setting at odds with real-world mixed traffic, where AVs run heterogeneous, vendor-specific ADSes. Migrating such coordination into a safety assurance layer is non-trivial, as ADSes are black boxes and mixed traffic is only partially controllable ( i.e., HDVs and pedestrians cannot be overridden, and safe AVs should not be). In summary, existing work lacks V2X-enabled scene-level safety assurance for mixed traffic with heterogeneous AVs. To the best of our knowledge, no existing work monitors scene-level hazards and jointly intervenes on unsafe AVs without modifying their underlying planning logic. To bridge this gap, we present C OSAFE , a modular and ADS-agnostic cooperative runtime safety assurance framework for mixed-traffic autonomous driving that extends runtime safety assurance from ego-centric takeover to scene-level cooperative intervention. Specifically, C OSAFE consists of three RSU-side modules, namely cooperative perception , hazard assessment ,and joint hazard mitigation , together with an AV-side control switch module. Within the coverage area of an RSU, the cooper-ative perception aggregates V2X information from participating AVs with RSU-side observations to construct the scene-level traffic information. Based on this information, the hazard as-sessment detects potential driving hazards, and identifies cor-responding unsafe AVs; and the joint hazard mitigation jointly generates safe reference trajectories for unsafe AVs. For each participating AV, the control switch either passes through the original ADS-generated planned trajectory if no intervention is needed, or executes the generated safe reference trajectory. We conduct large-scale experiments to evaluate the effective-ness and efficiency of C OSAFE in mixed-traffic environments. We integrate COSAFE with seven state-of-the-art ADSes spanning five families, and evaluate them on the InterDrive benchmark [36]. Our experiments demonstrate that C OSAFE 

effectively improves the driving score of AVs by 70.03%, while reducing safety violations by 87.80% on average, with runtime latency satisfying real-time constraints. In addition, C OSAFE 

accurately identifies unsafe AVs with an F 3 score of 0.930; and our ablation and mixed-deployment results demonstrate that each module contributes distinctly and that C OSAFE remains effective when coexisting AVs run heterogeneous ADSes. The main contributions of our work are as follows.

A. Autonomous Driving Systems 

ADSes provide the underlying planning logic of AVs, deter-mining how vehicles behave in dynamic traffic environments [9]. Existing ADSes are heterogeneous in their internal archi-tectures, including modular ADSes that separate perception, prediction, planning, and control [19], end-to-end ADSes that directly map sensor observations to trajectories or control commands [24, 30, 56], LLM-driven ADSes that incorporate language-based reasoning [48], and V2X-enabled cooperative ADSes that exploit shared perception [37] or negotiation information [36]. Despite their architectural differences, they share a functional interface that maps traffic observations to intended driving behaviors. In this work, we abstract an ADS ADS i as a black-box mapping from sensor observations oti and auxiliary planning information ati (e.g., target point and high-level maneuver command) to an ADS-generated planned trajectory τ ti at each discrete time step t. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

To address the scene-level safety assurance problem defined in Sec. II-C, we propose C OSAFE , a cooperative runtime safety assurance framework for heterogeneous ADSes in mixed-traffic environments, to identify unsafe AVs and generate coordinated safe reference trajectories at runtime. The key idea of C OSAFE 

is to extend runtime safety assurance from ego-centric takeover to scene-level cooperative intervention. Instead of assessing each AV in isolation, C OSAFE jointly evaluates the intentions of multiple AVs under shared scene-level information, identifies the unsafe AVs involved in potential violations, and coordinates their safe reference trajectories. AVs with safe intentions con-tinue to execute their ADS-generated planned trajectories, while only unsafe AVs are selected to override. C OSAFE is ADS-agnostic, which does not require modifying or retraining the native ADS, and only relies on the standardized V2X interfaces for state, intention, and scene-feature exchange. 

A. Framework Overview 

Fig. 2 shows the approach overview of C OSAFE . Overall, it is designed to preserve a scene-level safety invariant Φt

Then, the AV uploads its BEV feature BEV ti, vehicle state sti, and ADS intention, including τ ti and ati, to the RSU through V2X communication. On the RSU side, the cooperative perception aggregates the uploaded AV-side information with RSU-side observations to construct the scene-level information of the traffic environment and predict the future motions of observed traffic participants (see Sec. III-B). Based on the resulting scene-level information, the hazard assessment evaluates the driving hazards using a set of hazard oracles that capture different types of potential safety violations, and identifies the unsafe AV set Ut that requires intervention (see Sec. III-C). (see Sec. III-D). Finally, the RSU sends the generated safe reference trajectories back to the corresponding unsafe AVs. For an AV that does not receive a safe reference trajectory, the ADS-generated planned trajectory τ ti is passed through and executed by the vehicle controller. Receiving a safe reference trajectory indicates that the RSU has identified the AV as unsafe, and the control switch tracks the assigned trajectory ˆτ ti

by a reference-guided override to produce control commands that mitigate the potential driving hazards (see Sec. III-E). In summary, at each time step t, the hazard assessment identi-fies AVs whose intended trajectories may violate Φt

E. Control Switch 

To allow the safe reference trajectories {ˆτ ti }av i∈U t to take effect on unsafe AVs, each participating AV runs a con-trol switch module that is conditionally activated upon re-ceiving its assigned safe reference trajectory ˆτ ti from the RSU. Specifically, when no ˆτ ti is received, the AV’s original ADS-generated planned trajectory τ ti is passed through to the vehicle controller. Otherwise, the control switch uses ˆτ ti as the trajectory finally executed by the vehicle, and tracks it through our reference-guided override , implemented as a model predictive controller (MPC) that minimizes the tracking error to 

ˆτ ti over the MPC horizon under a kinematic bicycle model [21], with additional penalties on control magnitude and rate [38]. The core of the control switch is the pass-through-or-override logic conditioned on ˆτ ti , while the inner tracker is interchange-able. The MPC above is only a reference implementation, and any vendor-supplied controller ( e.g., PID, or learned policy) can substitute it without altering the RSU-side pipeline, preserving COSAFE ’s modular and ADS-agnostic design. We have implemented a prototype of C OSAFE with 7,959 lines of Python code. The source code and data of our work are available at [14]. To evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of C OSAFE , we design the following six research questions.  

> •

RQ1 Effectiveness Evaluation : How effective is C OSAFE in providing cooperative runtime safety assurance for various heterogeneous ADSes in mixed-traffic environments?  

> •

RQ2 Efficiency Evaluation : Can C OSAFE satisfy runtime latency constraints in mixed-traffic environments?  

> •

RQ3 Accuracy Evaluation : How accurately can the hazard assessment identify unsafe AVs?  

> •

RQ4 Ablation Study : How does each key module of COSAFE contribute to its overall effectiveness?  

> •

> •

A. Evaluation Setup 

Benchmark. We adopt the InterDrive benchmark [36], a re-cent closed-loop benchmark built on the V2Xverse [37] plat-form that systematically assesses multi-vehicle interaction in CARLA simulator [16]. InterDrive comprises 10 typical multi-vehicle scenario types with reference to the pre-crash typology of NHTSA [1], organized into three categories, i.e., InterDrive-IC where vehicles enter, meet, and exit an intersection from different directions, InterDrive-LM where vehicles merge into the same lane across parallel lanes, T-junctions, and highway ramps, and InterDrive-LC where vehicles traveling in parallel execute lane-change maneuvers that intersect adjacent vehicles’ trajectories. We use the NPC-enriched configuration [36], which yields 46 routes ( i.e., 18 for IC, 20 for LM, and 8 for LC) across 3 towns, each assigning 2 to 8 AVs on overlapping paths and populated with background vehicles, pedestrians, and cyclists. The driving task requires each AV to reach its destination while interacting safely with surrounding traffic participants. 

Target ADSes. We integrate C OSAFE with seven state-of-the-art ADSes spanning five families to evaluate its applicability across heterogeneous ADSes: (i) a modular ADS, Auto-ware [19]; (ii) three end-to-end ADSes, TCP [56], UniAD [24], and VAD [30]; (iii) an LLM-driven ADS, LMDrive [48]; (iv) a shared-perception cooperative ADS, CoDriving [37]; and (v) an LLM-mediated negotiation cooperative ADS, CoLMDriver [36]. For each AV, we run its native ADS with the publicly released official implementation, extracting the ADS-generated planned trajectory and auxiliary planning information from its public interfaces without modifying the underlying planning logic. In our prototype, all target ADSes share a single auxiliary BEV encoder and RSU-side fusion model, both pre-trained on DeepAccident [55], instead of per-ADS BEV adapters [42]. This strategy keeps BEV-alignment quality constant across ADSes, and thus the reported gains are attributable to C OSAFE ’s safety assurance. 

Baselines. We compare C OSAFE against A RGUS [53], the state-of-the-art ego-centric runtime safety assurance framework for end-to-end ADSes, applying it to TCP, UniAD, and VAD within its design scope. To the best of our knowledge, no publicly available safety assurance framework applies to the other four target ADSes ( i.e., LMDrive, CoDriving, CoLMDriver, and Autoware). For all seven target ADSes, we additionally report their native ADS baselines without assurance as comparison. 

Metrics. To measure C OSAFE ’s safety assurance effective-ness, we adopt two complementary metric groups, i.e., driving-performance metrics for the overall driving quality, and viola-tion counts for hazard prevention. Following A RGUS [53], we measure driving performance by four CARLA Leaderboard metrics [16], i.e., Route Completion (RC , the completion ratio of each route), Infraction Score (IS , a penalty factor starting at 1.0 and decreasing with each violation), Driving Score (DS =

RC · IS ), and Success Rate (SR , the fraction of routes com-pleted with a full-mark DS ). Within each route, we aggregate 

RC as the average, and IS as the product across all AVs. The final reported RC , IS , and DS are then averaged across the 46 routes. For hazard prevention, we count four types of safety violations corresponding to the four hazard oracles, i.e., vehicle collision ( Coll ), failure to obey stop signals ( Stop ), multi-vehicle deadlock ( Deadlock ), and vehicle stalling ( Stall ). 

RQ Setup. For RQ1 , we compare C OSAFE against the base-lines across the seven target ADSes separately, where each route deploys the same ADS for all AVs to isolate the per-ADS effect, and report the driving performance and violation counts. For RQ2 , we measure the per-time-step wall-clock latency of each module of C OSAFE over all routes, and report both the mean and P95 latency to characterize typical and tail behavior. For RQ3 , following A RGUS [53], we extract violation ground truth from the corresponding unprotected baseline runs using CARLA’s infraction events. We treat an intervention as neces-sary if it occurs within 3 seconds prior to a violation. Based on this, we count necessary interventions as true positives (TP), unnecessary interventions as false positives (FP), and violations not preceded by an intervention as false negatives (FN), and report precision = TP TP +FP , recall = TP TP +FN , and F 3 score to em-phasize recall over precision in safety-critical settings. For RQ4 , we conduct module-level ablation studies on TCP as a representative ADS. We create five variants of C OSAFE ,each replacing one key module with a simpler alternative, i.e., 

(i) w/o cooperative perception disables V2X-based AV infor-mation sharing, and uses only RSU-side observations, (ii) w/o deadlock & stall oracle keeps only the collision and stop signal oracles in the hazard assessment, isolating the contribution of the interaction-induced oracles introduced by C OSAFE , and (iii) w/o joint selection replaces BnB-based joint selection with one of three simpler mechanisms, leaving other modules unchanged ( i.e., per-AV greedy selection, where each unsafe AV independently selects its best candidate; deterministic priority inspired by AIM [17], where AVs take their best non-conflicting candidate in order of expected corridor entry, or brake; and randomized backoff, where each conflicting AV brakes for a random 1-8 time steps with a probability of 0.5). For RQ5 , unlike RQ1 where each route uses a single ADS type, the AVs within each route are assigned to TCP, LMDrive, and UniAD in rotation to simulate real-world roads with multiple vendors. These ADSes cover the end-to-end and LLM-driven families exhibiting different failure modes, and COSAFE reads only the V2X interface without identifying which ADS an AV runs. The three ADSes appear in balanced proportions across the 46 routes (31.3% TCP, 34.4% LMDrive, and 34.4% UniAD). The same AV-to-ADS mapping is used in both baseline and C OSAFE runs to ensure a fair comparison. For RQ6 , to simulate imperfect V2X communication, we inject three perturbations ( i.e., 250 ms delay, 500 ms delay, and 30% packet loss) into RSU-to-AV messages on TCP as a representative ADS, with all other modules unchanged. An AV that receives no safe reference trajectory executes its ADS-generated planned trajectory. 

Environment. We conduct all the experiments on Ubuntu 20.04.4 LTS servers with 4 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs, Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4310 @ 2.10GHz and 128GB memory. To mitigate the impact of randomness, all the experiments are conducted five times, and the average results are reported.

Breakdown of A RGUS . The residual failures of both A RGUS

In addition, the increased deadlocks under A RGUS expose the limitation of ego-centric takeover, where AVs yield independently without scene-level coordination, producing locally reasonable yet jointly incompatible decisions. Deadlock and Stall violations, most notably on VAD. These residual cases mainly arise when unsafe AVs are already too close for any forward-only candidate trajectories to resolve the conflict. Like most ADSes, C OSAFE does not include reverse motion in its action space, and thus the BnB search falls back to a conservative all-stop assignment. This safety-oriented fallback often converts imminent collisions into less severe deadlock or stall states, thereby substantially reducing collision violations on VAD while leaving some residual liveness violations. 

C. Efficiency Evaluation (RQ2) 

We evaluate the runtime efficiency of C OSAFE by profiling its per-time-step wall-clock latency. The four modules’ per-time-step latencies are 27.55 / 52.30 ms (mean / P95) for the co-operative perception, 1.91 / 4.60 ms for the hazard assessment, 50.25 / 80.42 ms for the joint hazard mitigation (over active-intervention time steps), and below 0.01 ms for the control switch, with a mean total of 79.72 ms per active-intervention cycle. The logic-heavy kernels ( e.g., BnB-based joint selection and conflict-graph construction) are accelerated via Cython [4]. To contextualize C OSAFE ’s runtime overhead, we compare it to the latency of seven state-of-the-art ADSes measured in our experiments. Across the ADSes, the mean per-time-step latency ranges from 168.6 ms (TCP, the fastest) to 4,765.6 ms (CoLM-Driver, the slowest), all of which significantly exceed C OSAFE ’s 

∼80 ms budget. Since C OSAFE mainly runs on the RSU side in parallel with each AV’s native ADS, its overhead is fully subsumed by the ADS’s own planning latency, and does not ex-tend the critical path. Therefore, C OSAFE delivers cooperative safety assurance while satisfying real-time constraints.

First, the benchmark selection may pose a threat to external validity. To mitigate this threat, we evaluate C OSAFE on Inter-Drive [36], the only publicly available closed-loop benchmark for V2X-enabled cooperative driving in mixed traffic, with its 10 scenario types aligned with the NHTSA pre-crash typology [1] for evaluation in real-world accident distributions. These NHTSA-aligned scenarios cover the major interaction patterns expected in real-world mixed traffic, providing evidence that COSAFE ’s safety assurance is not tied to a specific scenario. Second, the quality of cooperative perception, motion pre-diction, and BEV fusion may affect internal validity. C OSAFE 

handles perception uncertainty through conservative measures (e.g., bounding-box inflation in hazard oracles, a cooldown window against temporal flickering, and per-step re-assessment that corrects mis-predictions at the next observation). To isolate COSAFE ’s safety assurance contribution from ADS-specific BEV adapters, our prototype evaluates all target ADSes under a uniform RSU-side BEV encoder and fusion model pre-trained on DeepAccident [55]. The required interfaces are specified as data formats ( i.e., BEV features, vehicle states, ADS intentions, HD maps, and infrastructure states) rather than fixed perception implementations, and are compatible with established V2X co-operative perception [15, 37, 42, 55, 62]. Thus, the applicability of C OSAFE scales with future literature advances. Third, the diversity of driving hazards presents a potential threat, as our current implementation focuses on four representa-tive safety violations. Compared with prior ego-centric runtime assurance [52, 53], C OSAFE explicitly defines an interaction-induced deadlock oracle that is critical in mixed traffic where multiple AVs may make locally reasonable yet jointly incom-patible decisions. The modular design of C OSAFE allows addi-tional hazard oracles to be incorporated without changing the pass-through-or-override abstraction, e.g., map-rule violations such as illegal U-turns or restricted-lane entry. Specifically, a new oracle needs to (i) be computable from the scene-level information constructed by cooperative perception; (ii) output the AVs requiring intervention; (iii) fit the per-step hazard assessment budget; and (iv) be mitigable by trajectory-level override, with its check reused in candidate pruning if it also constrains candidate trajectories. While the current oracles cover a wide range of common safety violations, other hazards, 

e.g., sensor hardware faults or actuator failures, require separate fault models and are beyond the scope of this work. Finally, the deployment assumption underlying C OSAFE 

may affect real-world applicability. C OSAFE assumes an RSU-assisted prescriptive cooperation setting, where participating AVs exchange state, intention, and scene-level information with the RSU and execute the returned safe reference trajectories, which is consistent with the prescriptive cooperation class in SAE J3216 [2] and recent V2X-enabled cooperative driving platforms [7, 37]. R ELATED WORK 

Safety Assurance for ADSes. It aims to keep AVs within acceptable safety bounds by continuously monitoring driving hazards and intervening before potential safety violations occur [10, 44, 52, 53]. Prior studies mainly monitor driving hazards by detecting unexpected driving conditions, distributional shifts, low model confidence, or rule-based misbehavior [6, 23, 27, 45, 49, 50, 64]. However, these works are not designed to detect interaction-induced hazards such as deadlock that arise in mixed traffic, and focus on hazard detection rather than mitigation. Furthermore, various fallback strategies have been proposed to keep ADSes within safety bounds [10, 27, 35, 44, 60, 63]. Rule-based safety models like RSS define interpretable safety distances and response rules for automated driving [47], while emergency braking systems trigger conservative responses to avoid potential collisions [33, 65]. Runtime assurance frame-works like Simplex-Drive [10] and RTA-IR [44] adopt switch-ing architectures that transfer control to a verified safety con-troller upon predicted violations, and fallback policies [60] pre-scribe pull-over maneuvers in emergencies. However, they rely on predetermined fallback responses rather than synthesizing scene-aware safe trajectories that resolve specific hazards. Recent software engineering work has further studied runtime enforcement for ADS trajectories. REDriver [52] and Fix-Drive [51] modify ADS-generated planned trajectories to enforce specification compliance, while ARGUS [53] for-mulates runtime assurance as a hazard-aware takeover-and-recovery process for ADSes. While they demonstrate the benefit of adding a runtime safety layer over ADSes, their interventions remain ego-centric, with each ADS-controlled vehicle monitored and overridden independently and interaction-induced hazards in mixed traffic left unresolved. In contrast, COSAFE provides an ADS-agnostic cooperative runtime safety assurance framework that monitors scene-level hazards and jointly generates coordinated safe reference trajectories for multiple unsafe AVs in mixed traffic. 

Cooperative Autonomous Driving. It leverages V2X com-munication to extend the perception, prediction, and coordina-tion capability of individual AVs. Extensive work studies coop-erative perception to alleviate occlusion [15, 40, 42, 55, 57, 58]. End-to-end cooperative driving systems further integrate shared perception into closed-loop driving [37, 61, 62]. Beyond per-ception sharing, decentralized planning approaches let each AV cooperatively plan with neighbors [8, 68, 69]. LLM-mediated negotiation approaches such as CoLMDriver [36] further help multiple AVs exchange intentions and reach agreements [18, 20]. However, they mainly enhance the information available to each ADS, while the driving decisions are still produced by the independent driving policies and may remain unsafe. Another line of work focuses on centralized multi-vehicle planning [7, 25, 26, 39, 41]. They often assume controllable CAV fleets, homogeneous cooperation protocols, or direct re-placement of the native driving policy. In contrast, C OSAFE is not a centralized driving planner, but an ADS-agnostic runtime safety assurance layer that selectively overrides only unsafe AVs while preserving each AV’s native ADS in normal operation. VII. C ONCLUSIONS 

We propose and implement C OSAFE , an ADS-agnostic co-operative runtime safety assurance framework for mixed-traffic autonomous driving. Our large-scale experiments on the InterDrive benchmark with seven state-of-the-art ADSes demon-strate the effectiveness, efficiency, and generality of C OSAFE .VIII. D ATA AVAILABILITY
