# Genie Sim Benchmark: Real-to-Sim Fidelity and Evaluation 

Data-Driven Simulation Fidelity and Real-World Robot Evaluation Di Yang ∗ Da Huang ∗ Yang Lv ∗ Fan Yang Yi Lu Zheyuan Xing Linting Guo Jia’en Shen Wenjun Sun Maoqing Yao † Qian Wang †

* First authors † Corresponding authors 

Abstract— We evaluate Genie Sim Benchmark through paired real–simulation tasks and complementary system-identification studies. Across 22 robot–task pairs spanning 16 task names, task-specific π0.5 policies fine-tuned on real-robot data achieve a descriptive Score correlation of 0.9686 (MAE 0.0405; RMSE 0.0520), with simulation–real differences from −0.110 to +0.080. G2 crsB experiments assess long-horizon closed-loop position and joint-side torque agreement, while five representative object cases examine identification and cross-engine contact transfer. A separate 32.6503 s teleoperation trajectory, excluded from mechanical identification and servo-gain search, evaluates external position targets through simulated motion-controller command shaping and a calibrated position servo. Across both arms, per-joint position RMSE spans 0.00614–0.03522 rad and current-derived torque RMSE spans 0.73545–4.00099 N ·m. Native PhysX adaptations recover three recorded interaction tasks, with case-dependent alignment gains. These results support task-specific simulation evaluation through joint assessment of task outcomes and physical-response consistency. G ENIE SIM BENCHMARK : P ROTOCOL , C OVERAGE AND REAL –S IM EVALUATION 

A. Genie Sim Benchmark: platform and capability dimen-sions 

Genie Sim Benchmark is an embodied-model evaluation platform built around a systematic, multidimensional as-sessment framework [27]. It uses high-fidelity simulation evaluation closely aligned with real-robot performance to help developers identify model strengths and weaknesses and guide continuous improvement. The platform reports 78 simulation tasks across four capability dimensions. Per-dimension results, completed substeps and failure-stage di-agnostics help developers localize limitations and prioritize model and training-data improvements. 

a) Platform capability dimensions: Instruction Fol-lowing. Colour, quantity, shape, size, category, logic and common-sense instructions test whether a model selects the intended target and executes the requested action. 

Spatial Reasoning. Absolute position, relative relations, size and ordinal ordering, stacking and spatial alignment test the interpretation of object arrangements. 

Robustness. Changes in background, lighting, material, robot initial state, camera pose, image quality and instruction wording probe performance under perturbations. 

General Manipulation. Door opening, pouring, scooping, stocking, sorting, table clearing and bimanual coordination test the composition of atomic skills in multi-step tasks. The platform description organizes its 78 tasks into these four capability dimensions and records completed substeps and failure stages. It describes diverse samples, domain ran-domization, separate training and test sets, and in-distribution and out-of-distribution tests as evaluation design mecha-nisms. 

B. Real2Sim task selection and embodiment coverage 

To examine real–simulation consistency across robot em-bodiments and end effectors, the evaluation covers G1 Om-nipicker, G2 Swiftpicker, G2 Wuji and Arx X5. The newly added G2 and Arx tasks cover rigid-object manipulation and deformable textile manipulation. Figure 2 presents representative real–simulation scene pairs for all four configurations, illustrating the robot embodiments, ob-ject arrangements and interaction contexts used in the exper-iments. The evaluation spans five task families. Selection &reasoning tests instruction-conditioned target selection and grasp execution. Placement & sorting tests object trans-port and release into containers, drawers or onto supports. 

Bimanual handover examines coordinated transfer between hands, while Stacking examines alignment and stable place-ment. Textile manipulation extends the evaluation to towels and clothing, whose deformation changes the object con-figuration during manipulation. Together, these tasks assess real–simulation correspondence across target selection, rigid-object contact and deformable-object interaction. Table 1 summarizes the complete task coverage; Figure 2 illustrates representative scenes from this evaluation. Per-task Scores and test quantities are provided in Table B1, and the matched identification comparisons are reported in Table B2.

Real2Sim connects benchmark task construction with physical-response validation: define paired tasks, instantiate robot and interacting assets, reproduce recorded commands, identify robot and object response, and evaluate simula-tion against real observations and task Scores. This report presents complementary evidence from G2 crsB system iden-tification, five signature-asset object studies and 22 paired robot–task evaluations. 

A. Real2Sim construction and execution 

The object-replay study uses the full G2 model, initializing robot and mimic joints from real feedback and applying 22 joint-position targets and two gripper-position targets. Commands originate from HAL and end-effector command messages; the H5 action channel is not substituted. Recorded targets are held at approximately 100 Hz while physics advances in 1 ms substeps. Objects remain free rigid bodies throughout integration: there is no grasp attachment, frame-wise object-pose overwrite or inverse-kinematics rewrite. Newton in this study uses SolverMuJoCo / MuJoCo-Warp to load the frozen MJB. MuJoCo and MJWarp share contact semantics; PhysX provides a distinct contact implementation. Table 2 identifies the three execution stacks used for the object comparisons. 

B. Fixed-parameter replay and evidence alignment 

The object study fixes robot execution settings while comparing candidate object parameters and execution stacks. Command response, object behavior, stage events and task Score are measured separately. Five signature-asset record-ings examine representative interaction families; 22 bench-mark pairs evaluate task outcomes. The excitation-family robot diagnostics compare joint-side sensor torque with sensor-observer predictions. The independent teleoperation replay instead compares simulated motor-side joint torque with current-derived joint torque. External position targets pass through simulated motion-controller (MC) command shaping and the calibrated position servo, with mechanical parameters and servo gains fixed. Each study retains its own experimental units and denominator. The object replay retains its own G2 simulation priors and Swiftpicker settings; Table D1 records the object-replay and excitation-family robot-diagnostic configurations. 

C. What is measured? 

We distinguish real-observation alignment , cross-engine trajectory agreement , and physical task completion . The observation error is RMSE = √[mean( ∥psim − pobs ∥2)], measured in mm for 3D points or px for image points. It is evaluated at the valid observation timestamps. Neither the feature points nor their visibility are common across tasks, so their errors are not pooled into a single accuracy score. RGB-D centres are model-assisted observations rather than motion-capture ground truth. Native simulated contact forces use different readback semantics, and no real contact-force reference is supplied. The “held-out” time windows are later segments of the same recording; whole-trajectory feasibility gates and development inspection mean they are not blind independent tests. IV. S YSTEM IDENTIFICATION 

Benchmark fidelity depends on two separately testable responses: robot command execution and object interaction under a fixed robot and scene configuration. The independent G2 crsB teleoperation replay applies external position targets through simulated MC command shaping and a calibrated position servo, evaluating position and current-derived joint torque with fixed dynamics and gains. The excitation-family sensor diagnostics and G2–Swiftpicker object replays retain their own frozen configurations (Appendices C and D). These studies provide complementary execution- and interaction-level evidence under their respective configurations.

The G2 crsB study identifies both seven-joint arms using friction, sine and Fourier experiments. Sections IV-A.1–IV-A.3 describe the model, staged estimation and recorded-data reconstruction; Section IV-A.4 tests an unused teleoperation recording. Thus the model has 70 inertial, seven armature, fourteen friction and seven bias unknowns: 98 scalars per arm. These 98 coordinates are not all independently observable from joint torque. Rigid-body regressors contain structural dependencies, while limited excitation can further correlate inertia, armature and friction columns. A simultaneous fit therefore cannot generally recover a unique full physical parameter vector. We separate the experiments and regularize physically feasible estimates; this reduces coupling without claiming unique identification of every link property [12–16]. Both dynamic stages require each link’s pseudo-inertia matrix to be positive semidefinite, constrain mass to 50%– 150% of its original URDF/CAD reference, and enforce nonnegative armature/friction; bias signs are unrestricted.

3) Findings and scope: Task recovery and identification gain are distinct outcomes. The matched native compar-isons show that feasible contact execution can be restored for both baseline and fitted models. Scope of the evidence. These five single-episode stud-ies demonstrate recorded-command object identification and contact diagnostics. Repeated simulations and temporal sub-sets of those recordings do not establish independent-episode generalization. MuJoCo/MJWarp agreement involves related backends; fixed mass priors, approximate geometry, effective penetration and strong damping also limit physical-parameter interpretation. The current results do not establish a causal reduction in benchmark task-score discrepancy.

Future work. VLM-based priors and agent-selected in-teractions are proposed extensions, not yet validated in this study. V. C ONCLUSION 

Task-level consistency. We present Genie Sim Benchmark through paired real–simulation evaluation. This report de-tails selected robot- and object-level identification evidence. Across 22 robot–task pairs spanning 16 task names and four configurations, the evaluated Scores exhibit strong correspon-dence, with a descriptive Pearson correlation of 0.9686 and signed simulation–real differences from −0.110 to +0.080. These results demonstrate close task-level correspondence within the evaluated conditions and support the use of simu-lation as an informative reference for real-robot performance. 

Physical-response evidence. Beyond task Scores, this re-port examines the physical responses underlying benchmark interactions. G2 crsB identification and excitation-family closed-loop diagnostics assess coupled robot motion through position and joint-side sensor torque. A separate 32.6503 s teleoperation trajectory, excluded from mechanical iden-tification and servo-gain search, evaluates external position targets through simulated MC command shaping, the cali-brated position servo and fixed robot dynamics. Across both arms, per-joint position RMSE spans 0.00614–0.03522 rad and current-derived torque RMSE spans 0.73545–4.00099 N·m. This single-recording validation supports transfer of the combined execution model to the evaluated motion; it does not isolate the contribution of mechanical identification or establish benchmark task-level gains. Five signature-asset studies examine representative grasping, carrying, contact and handover mechanics. Fixed-parameter replay across Mu-JoCo, MJWarp and PhysX exposes the influence of object parameters and contact representations, while native PhysX adaptations recover recorded interactions. Together, these studies provide complementary evidence at the execution, interaction and task levels. 

Evaluation value. A central contribution is the connec-tion between real-data-driven calibration and measurable simulation behavior. Task outcomes establish whether an interaction succeeds; motion, torque and object observations characterize how closely its physical response follows real-ity. Evaluating both distinguishes successful execution from trajectory alignment and makes model differences actionable for simulator development. 3. Historical transfer failure and native PhysX recovery 

Historical question and observation. Can an effective contact model fitted in MuJoCo preserve a sponge grasp after fixed transfer? The G2 source recording contains 92 RGB-D centre observations over 9.210 s, from one real episode (4247312). After a 120-candidate MuJoCo search, full-replay centre RMSE falls from 34.99 to 20.84 mm in MuJoCo and 59.85 to 21.77 mm in MJWarp. Working hypothesis. The fitted effective compliant con-tact is not represented equivalently in the PhysX transfer. The source path does not express MuJoCo solref/solimp equivalently. This supports investigating contact representa-tion and parameter mapping; it does not uniquely identify the cause. Geometry, initialization, robot/observation calibration 22 and numerical differences remain potential contributors.
