## Abstract 

End-to-end autonomous driving systems increasingly rely on gen-erating multiple trajectory candidates and selecting one for exe-cution. In this setting, a central challenge is no longer only candi-date generation, but how to evaluate candidates according to their long-horizon consequences under uncertainty. Existing methods still mainly use deterministic point scores, heuristic subscores, or single-outcome evaluations, which makes it difficult to capture de-layed failures , tail risk , and ranking instability under plausible arXiv:2611.13790v1 [cs.RO] 10 Nov 2026 MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil Zhao et al., Yu Zhao, Hongyi Duan, Ming Yin, Hongyuan Zhang, Fan Yang, Hongzhi Wang, and Xiubo Liang 

future perturbations. We present RiSE-Drive , a framework for risk-sensitive long-horizon candidate evaluation in end-to-end au-tonomous driving. RiSE-Drive first performs candidate-conditioned latent future rollout, then estimates long-horizon outcome distri-butions instead of single scalar scores, and finally ranks candidates using a tail-risk-aware and perturbation-robust decision criterion. This reformulates candidate scoring from deterministic oracle-style evaluation into distributional outcome assessment with explicit risk control. As a result, the evaluator is encouraged to prefer candidates that are not only strong under nominal conditions, but also reliable under long-horizon rollout and safety-critical deviations. Experi-ments on the NAVSIM-v1 and NAVSIM-v2 benchmarks , as well as the closed-loop HUGSIM benchmark , show that RiSE-Drive achieves state-of-the-art performance and significantly improves the robustness of candidate selection.

## 1 Introduction 

End-to-end autonomous driving aims to map raw multi-view ob-servations directly to a safe, rule-compliant, and route-progressive future ego trajectory in a unified learned planner [6, 13, 15, 47]. Modern systems increasingly adopt a generate-and-select paradigm, where a proposal module enumerates multiple feasible futures and a selector chooses one for execution [11, 18, 20, 25]. Under behavior-grounded evaluation such as NAVSIM and NAVSIM-v2 [3, 8], the central challenge is therefore not only to generate plausible can-didates, but to evaluate which candidate remains best under long-horizon interactions and future uncertainty. Among recent ResNet-34-based planners, MindDrive [40] is a strong representative of richer future reasoning. Yet, at the surface level, even strong planners can remain brittle in safety-critical long-tail scenes and perturbed rollouts: several candidates may receive similar nominal scores, while the selected one fails after down-stream interactions. The deeper reason is that candidate quality is not a deterministic scalar, but a distribution of long-horizon conse-quences induced by uncertain future conditions. Existing methods still largely compress this uncertainty into point scores, heuristic subscores, or single-outcome estimates, without explicit candidate-conditioned counterfactual rollout and lower-tail risk modeling. As a result, hidden tail failures remain invisible to the selector, and ranking becomes unstable exactly when robustness matters most. We address this limitation with RiSE-Drive , a risk-sensitive long-horizon candidate evaluator for end-to-end driving. RiSE-Drive leaves the upstream perception and proposal-generation pipeline unchanged and modifies only the evaluation stage. For each candidate, it predicts a compact set of future-condition branches, performs candidate-conditioned latent consequence propagation, decodes one long-horizon outcome sample for each branch, esti-mates an outcome distribution through ordered quantiles, and ranks candidates using a criterion that combines the mean, lower-tail Con-ditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) [36], and cross-condition instability. This design directly targets the root cause above: branchwise roll-out captures delayed interaction effects, distributional estimation exposes hidden tail failures, and CVaR-based ranking favors tra-jectories that remain reliable not only in expectation but also in the lower tail under plausible future deviations. Instantiated on top of DrivoR [18], RiSE-Drive is orthogonal to backbone scaling and isolates the value of better candidate evaluation. Our contributions are summarized as follows: (1) We identify a central evaluation mismatch in candidate-based end-to-end driving: candidate quality should be modeled as a distribution over future-condition-dependent long-horizon outcomes, rather than reduced to a single deterministic score. (2) We propose RiSE-Drive , a unified evaluator that combines future-condition modeling, candidate-conditioned latent con-sequence propagation, distributional outcome estimation, and CVaR-based risk-sensitive ranking to make trajectory selection explicitly tail-risk aware. (3) RiSE-Drive achieves state-of-the-art results on NAVSIM-v1 [8], NAVSIM-v2 [3], and HUGSIM [55]. Under a matched DrivoR setup [18], it improves PDMS from 93.1 to 94.2, EPDMS from 48.3 to 51.3, and average HD-Score from 35.7 to 39.5, while consistently improving safety-critical and closed-loop robustness on hard and perturbation-heavy settings. 

## 2 Related Work 

End-to-end autonomous driving planning. Viewed chrono-logically, the development of end-to-end planning is less a story of ever-heavier perception backbones than a redefinition of the planner’s output space. Early systems such as VAD, TransFuser, UniAD, and PARA-Drive established the viability of unified plan-ners built on learned scene representations, stronger sensor fu-sion, and planning-oriented supervision [6, 13, 15, 47]. The next bottleneck, however, was objective mismatch: optimizing a single demonstrated trajectory encourages an average-case imitation pol-icy, even though urban driving is fundamentally set-valued and often admits multiple near-optimal behaviors. This motivated a second wave that recast planning as sequence modeling or density estimation, including autoregressive methods [5, 10], probabilistic or generative planners [4, 30, 53], and diffusion/flow-based formu-lations [16, 28, 32, 39, 44, 49]. In this regime, the decisive question is not merely whether a model can output a plausible future, but whether its decision space preserves sufficient behavioral support to remain reliable when multiple futures are simultaneously feasi-ble. NAVSIM and NAVSIM-v2 make this shift explicit, since their behavior-grounded metrics reward safety, progress, compliance, RiSE-Drive: Risk-Sensitive Long-Horizon Candidate Evaluation for End-to-End Autonomous Driving MM ’26, November 10–14, 2026, Rio de Janeiro, Brazil 

and robustness under perturbation rather than mere open-loop trajectory closeness [3, 8, 31, 52]. 

Candidate generation, trajectory scoring, and selection. 

An increasingly influential design pattern formulates E2E plan-ning as maintaining a candidate set and learning a selector over it. Early structured planners such as SparseDrive already expose this decomposition through sparse scene representations and explicit proposal reasoning [42], while Hydra-MDP and Hydra-MDP++ make multimodal candidate generation a first-class objective via hydra-distillation [20, 25]. Under NAVSIM-style evaluation [3, 8], this decomposition becomes even more consequential: the main difficulty is often not producing nominally plausible trajectories, but deciding which candidate is safest and most decision-optimal under safety, progress, and rule-compliance trade-offs. Accordingly, TransDiffuser emphasizes the support quality and decorrelation of the candidate set [16]; WoTE and World4Drive rank candidates through future-aware world modeling [23, 54]; and DriveSuprim, GTRS, ZTRS, and DrivoR make trajectory scoring itself a primary design axis [18, 26, 27, 50]. Among them, DrivoR is a particularly relevant baseline: it couples efficient transformer-based perception with disentangled proposal generation and oracle-supervised sub-score prediction, showing that lightweight proposal-and-selection pipelines can be highly competitive on NAVSIM [18]. Importantly, DrivoR is not a plain point predictor—it produces multiple propos-als and learns to rank them—but its proposal set is still generated by a one-shot deterministic decoder from fixed learned queries under winner-takes-all supervision, and the selector operates with-out explicit action-conditioned rollout of future scene responses, forcing long-horizon consequences to be inferred from the current compressed scene representation alone [18]. As a result, when sev-eral candidates are locally plausible but diverge mainly through downstream interactions, selection quality can still be bottlenecked by limited counterfactual consequence modeling. Related proposal-centric formulations such as ComDrive and iPad further reinforce that modern E2E planning is increasingly governed by candidate coverage, scoring fidelity, and risk-sensitive selection rather than raw trajectory regression [11, 43].

## 4 Method 4.1 Overview 

RiSE-Drive leaves the upstream perception and proposal generation pipeline unchanged and modifies only the candidate-evaluation stage. As summarized in Fig. 2, it predicts a small set of future-condition branches for each scene, rolls each candidate under each branch to obtain branch-level long-horizon outcome samples, ag-gregates these samples into a candidate-level outcome distribution, and finally ranks candidates with a risk-sensitive criterion based on the mean, lower-tail CVaR, and cross-condition instability.

Compared with NAVSIM-style evaluation, HUGSIM more directly measures long-horizon stability, recovery ability, and robustness to compounding closed-loop errors. 

## 5.2 Comparison with State-of-the-Art 

Tables 1–3 compare RiSE-Drive with recent end-to-end planners on NAVSIM-v1 [8], NAVSIM-v2 navhard split [3], and HUGSIM [55]. The compared methods span lightweight CNN backbones, stronger vision encoders such as InternImage-Tiny [45], V2-99 [19], and DINOv2 ViT-S [35], as well as larger VLM/VLA backbones such as Emu3-8B [46] and InternVL3-2B [57]. RiSE-Drive uses the same backbone and proposal generator as DrivoR [18]; the comparison against DrivoR therefore isolates the effect of long-horizon, distri-butional, and risk-sensitive candidate evaluation. 

NAVSIM-v1. Table 1 reports results on the navtest split. RiSE-Drive achieves the best PDMS of 94.2, improving over DrivoR by 1.1 points. Under the matched ViT-S setup, it raises NC from 98.9 to 99.5 and TTC from 96.2 to 98.4 while maintaining perfect comfort; EP decreases slightly from 89.1 to 88.7, indicating a safer and slightly more conservative selection policy. 

NAVSIM-v2 navhard-two-stage . Table 2 shows that RiSE-Drive achieves the best EPDMS of 51.3, outperforming DrivoR by 3.0 points and the strongest previous result in the table by 3.2 points. The gain is concentrated on Stage-II safety terms, including NC (90.2 →91.5), DDC (91.9 →94.6), and TTC (88.0 →91.3), while the modest EP reduction (70.0 →68.7) again reflects more cautious deci-sions under perturbation. 

HUGSIM.. Table 3 evaluates fully closed-loop transfer on HUGSIM. RiSE-Drive achieves the best average RC and HD-Score of 50.1 and 39.5, exceeding DrivoR by 0.3 and 3.8 points, respectively. The gain is largest on the hard and extreme subsets, where HD-Score rises from 18.8 to 22.7 and from 32.5 to 39.2, indicating stronger long-horizon robustness under compounding errors. Overall, the three benchmarks consistently show that, once a strong proposal set is available, improving candidate evaluation yields larger gains than backbone scaling alone. 

## 5.3 Ablation Study 

Unless otherwise stated, all ablations keep the DrivoR encoder and proposal generator fixed, so the results isolate the contribution of the proposed evaluator. We study three aspects: component-wise contribution, parameter overhead, and sensitivity to the future-branch and quantile design. We denote the original DrivoR by 

B-I , and a reduced baseline that replaces its scoring decoder with a lightweight direct score head by B-II . Starting from B-II, we progressively add FCG (Future-Condition Generator), CTE (candi-date trajectory-level encoder), SLE (step-level encoder), LCP (long-horizon consequence propagation), DOE (distributional outcome estimation), and RSR (risk-sensitive ranking). 

Component-wise ablation. Table 4 shows steady gains as the eval-uator is built up from the reduced baseline. Future conditioning and trajectory encoding recover much of the gap introduced by simpli-fying the original scorer, while explicit long-horizon consequence propagation yields the clearest benefit on the harder NAVSIM-v2 setting. The final improvements from DOE and RSR show that distri-butional prediction and risk-sensitive ranking are complementary rather than interchangeable. 

Parameter overhead. Table 5 shows that RiSE-Drive improves all three aggregate benchmarks with only a marginal increase in parameter count over DrivoR. The gain is therefore structural, not a consequence of brute-force model scaling.

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented RiSE-Drive, a risk-sensitive evaluator for candidate-based end-to-end autonomous driving. The central premise of this work is that, once a planner already provides a strong set of feasible trajectory proposals, the remaining bottleneck is no longer only candidate coverage, but how those candidates are judged under long-horizon uncertainty. Existing selectors largely compress candi-date quality into deterministic point scores or heuristic sub-scores, which can obscure delayed failures and hidden lower-tail risk. RiSE-Drive addresses this limitation by modeling candidate quality as a distribution over future-condition-dependent long-horizon out-comes. Concretely, it predicts a compact set of future-condition branches, performs candidate-conditioned latent consequence prop-agation, estimates ordered outcome quantiles, and ranks trajectories with a risk-sensitive criterion that combines the mean, lower-tail CVaR, and cross-condition instability. This formulation makes candidate selection explicitly sensitive to tail failures rather than only nominal performance. As a result, RiSE-Drive prefers trajectories that remain reliable not only on average, but also under plausible downstream deviations and interaction-heavy rollouts. Across NAVSIM-v1, NAVSIM-v2, and HUGSIM, as well as qualitative analysis on safety-critical NavSafe scenar-ios, RiSE-Drive consistently improves robustness under a matched DrivoR setup, with especially clear gains on hard, perturbation-heavy, and closed-loop evaluations. These results suggest that ro-bust end-to-end driving depends not only on stronger perception backbones or larger models, but also on principled long-horizon candidate evaluation. We hope this perspective encourages future work on tighter integration between proposal generation, conse-quence modeling, and risk-sensitive decision making.

## A.2 Future-Condition Branch Construction 

RiSE-Drive uses 𝐿 = 4 reference conditions to construct branch targets (Section 4.6). We pair two motion measurements from the same training-log window. Longitudinal pressure is the negative of the lead vehicle’s longitudinal acceleration. Lateral approach is the lateral velocity of a same-direction adjacent vehicle, signed positive toward the ego corridor. We first bisect the windows by the empirical rank of longitudinal pressure, then bisect each half by the within-group rank of lateral approach. The four conditions in Table A.1 use low and high levels relative to the training logs, with a separate lateral threshold in each longitudinal group. For each condition, we choose the observed pair closest to the group’s componentwise median, measuring distance after scaling both quantities by their training-set interquartile ranges.
