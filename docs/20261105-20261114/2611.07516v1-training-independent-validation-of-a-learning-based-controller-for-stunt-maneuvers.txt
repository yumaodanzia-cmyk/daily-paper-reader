# Training-Independent Validation of a Learning-Based Controller for Stunt Maneuvers 

Kiruthiga Chandra Shekar 1, Aliasghar Arab 2, and Jingang Yi 3

Abstract — Learned controllers for vehicle maneuvers are usually validated under the conditions and objective used to train them, where they appear highly reliable, yet the parameters that change on real roads are often not those the model was trained on. We argue that validation must be independent of training: it should vary physical parameters that training kept fixed and use acceptance criteria absent from the training objective. A learning-based control policy, G-Maneuver, with 90.11% success is tested for stunt-level maneuvers. A looser terminal-heading score ranked the worst setting as the best. Because real stunt tests are safety-critical, a simulation-to-reality-to-simulation (Sim2Real2Sim) loop uses the simulation to select safe zones for real-vehicle trials with 16.9% acceptance. Automotive safety analysis frameworks are used to identify parameters for validation using coverage tests. We demonstrated that the kinematic stunt-driving indicators, namely heading change, sideslip, and speed-normalized yaw rate, match between simulation and the real world, while force and roll indicators do not, which identifies the indicators that can support safe deployment. A video of the experiments is available at https://youtu.be/dN1Oes8pNIo .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Braking alone has a limited ability to avoid collisions. For a given closing speed and distance there is a boundary beyond which no braking profile can prevent impact, while a maneuver with rapid yaw motion can still steer the vehicle clear [1], [2]. Aggressive evasive maneuvers are therefore considered as the last available option in this part of the avoidance envelope, which is why they are studied as an active safety feature. The stunt J-turn maneuver with an extreme 180 ◦ spin while moving reverse as one of the most dynamically demanding maneuver of this family is demonstrated in Fig. 1. The J-turn is a challenging maneuver to conduct because it is a chain of phases, and its outcome depends mainly on when the controller switches from one phase to the next. Once the vehicle starts full-lock steering at a given reverse speed, for example, the heading at which stabilization begins is largely fixed, and a late or early switch cannot be undone. Computing these switching instants from a vehicle model is difficult because the tires operate at their friction limit, where tire models lose accuracy and a reference trajectory is hard to construct [3], [4]. Reinforcement learning [5], optimization-based motion planning [3], and agentic large language model (LLM) control [6] have all produced working J-turn controllers, and learned policies now also operate at the handling limit in racing [7] and drifting [8]. The instructed reinforcement learning approach formu-lated the J-turn as a sequence of dynamically distinct phases learned under safety constraints [5], and ManeuverGPT kept this phase structure but replaced gradient-based training with LLM agents that revise the phase commands in natural language after each simulated trial [6]. Classical vehicle dynamics describes the coupled lateral, yaw, and load-transfer effects that govern such transient handling, including secondary effects such as engine gyroscopic coupling [4], [9]. Our earlier work evaluated evasive maneuvers as a decision problem, asking whether and when to start one [10], [11]. The present paper takes that decision as given and asks how well the maneuver is executed afterward. Scenario-based testing is the dominant approach to au-tonomous vehicle safety assessment, because exhaustive real-world driving is impractical [12], [13]. A scenario is built from road geometry, traffic participants, and environmental conditions, and the vehicle is scored on outcomes such as collision avoidance; benchmarks such as CommonRoad standardize the scenario format [14], and candidate scenarios can be extracted from recorded driving [15]. Coverage-based testing adds a measure of completeness. It describes all possi-ble scenarios by a set of parameters and reports how much of that parameter space has been tested, rather than how many scenarios a library contains. Directed simulation budgets toward parameter regions likely to expose unsafe behavior [16], and several works define explicit multi-dimensional coverage metrics [17]–[19]. A trained adversarial vehicles can make safety-critical events more frequent, which reduces the required testing effort by orders of magnitude [20]. In all of these works the parameters describe the traffic scenario, such as traffic density, the trajectories of other road users, and obstacle placement. None of them asks whether a single maneuver, executed alone, remains acceptable across the physical conditions that govern it, such as road friction and the initial state of the vehicle. Hazard analysis complements coverage. Safety standards give researchers and industry a common language for hazards and acceptance evidence, so results expressed in their terms can be compared and reused by other teams. ISO 26262 ad-dresses risk from hardware and software faults [21], whereas ISO 21448, the Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF) standard, addresses insufficient performance without any fault [22]. SOTIF fits a learned controller: when the policy fails on a surface or from an initial state it was never tuned for, nothing has malfunctioned, so neither fault-based analysis nor the training score detects the failure. System-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) expresses hazards as unsafe control actions, and cascading it with scenario gener-ation turns hazards into executable tests [23]. We follow the same chain, from physical hazard to unsafe control action to test parameter (Table I), and end it in a pass or fail acceptance test rather than a safety integrity level, because our goal is evidence about a controller rather than product certification. This paper addresses how such a controller should be validated. A learned policy is usually evaluated under the conditions and objective used to train it, where it appears highly reliable. Training, however, fixes a small set of parameters, typically one road surface and one initial state, while friction, weather, and the initial state of the vehicle change on real roads. The paper makes three main contribu-tions. First, it defines a validation protocol independent of training that judges each trial by completion, measured only while the controller is active, and by recoverability when control is handed back. Second, it uses automotive safety analysis frameworks to derive the coverage parameters from the physical failure modes of the maneuver. Third, across 1,152 CARLA trials it shows that acceptance falls to 16.9%, identifies the conditions responsible. A Sim2Real2Sim loop is designed to simulate large-scale tests on the cloud to select safe zones for real-vehicle stunt tests. II. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

D. Test Grid and Failure Region 

By coverage we mean the set of test conditions at which the policy is evaluated. The test dimensions fall into three groups. Physical parameters change the plant and were fixed during training: the weather W ∈ {Clear, Cloudy, Fog, Rain }, which sets the road friction μ ∈ { 0.9, 0.9, 0.85 , 0.6}

and shrinks the friction circle (4), and the pre-maneuver wait tw ∈ { 0.5, 2.5, 4.5, 6.5} s between placing the vehicle in the scene and starting the maneuver, which determines how settled the initial state x(0) is. Schedule parameters are the thresholds Vr ∈ {6, 10 , 14 , 18 } m/s and ψg ∈{75 ◦, 90 ◦, 105 ◦}, which set the phase-switching instants that determine ∆ψJ ; varying them shows how tolerant the out-come is to a switch that happens earlier or later than training intended. Control parameters should not affect the maneuver and check the test setup: the time of day Td ∈ {day, night }, which only changes lighting, and the post-maneuver follow time th ∈ {0, 5, 10 } s, which only changes how long the run continues after hand-off. We therefore select the test points adap-tively: based on the outcomes observed so far, the method chooses which values of the test parameters to evaluate next and how finely to step along each parameter range. The adaptation concerns only the test design. The policy under test and its controller are unchanged in every trial, so the method is an adaptive coverage strategy, not adaptive control. Its point selection is inspired by linear programming. In a linear program, the optimum of a linear objective over a convex feasible region always lies at a vertex, so the search can move along the vertices and edges of the region instead of visiting its interior. Maneuver acceptance is not linear, but it behaves similarly in one useful respect: physical failures tend to be driven by the extremes of the parameter ranges, such as the highest reverse speed or the earliest start. Nonlinear effects can still create local minima and maxima inside the space, which a vertex search alone would miss. The method therefore starts from the boundaries of the test space and then searches the rest of the space for possible failures. The search runs in four stages. It first evaluates every vertex of the space, that is, every combination of the lowest and highest level of each ordered dimension, for every weather condition, since weather has no natural order. It then refines the edges by reducing the step along a parameter range. Let E ⊆ G be the set of evaluated points and ek aone-level step along axis k. Whenever p and p + n ek are both in E, with n > 1 and A(p)̸ = A(p + n ek), the method evaluates the point halfway between them, 

pm = p + ⌊n/ 2⌋ ek, (12) and repeats until every such disagreement is resolved to adjacent levels, which locates the failure boundary ∂F along each edge. Third, it probes the interior at the central levels of every dimension to detect local extrema that the vertices cannot reveal, and it refines around these probes with the same bisection rule. Finally, it traces the safe zones: it evaluates every unevaluated neighbor of each accepted point until every accepted point is enclosed by evaluated points, which follows ∂F into the interior. The second and fourth stages are repeated until no new point is added. We measure the coverage of E in two ways. We evaluate learning-based maneuvers in CARLA [24], an open, high-fidelity urban driving simulator with tunable vehicle and tire-road parameters, and on the autonomous vehicle test-bed shown in Fig. 6. All simulated trials are run in CARLA. To evaluate the adaptive coverage method against ground truth, we also ran the exhaustive grid once, which gives 1,152 trials and 96 for each combination of Vr and ψg ; the results in Section IV use this exhaustive set. Each trial logs position, velocity, attitude, yaw rate, steering, controller phase, and collision status at every frame. A. The Validation Criterion Changes the Conclusion 

The choice of criterion is not a detail. The built-in score of our simulation test harness measures the heading change over the whole run, including the time after hand-off, accepts 

180 ◦ ± 25 ◦, and requires that no collision flag is raised. It reports 156 successes, but only 80 of them pass our acceptance test. Of the other 76, 64 never completed the reversal during the maneuver, most of them stopping near 

158 ◦, and 12 completed it but were not settled at hand-off. Conversely, it rejects 115 trials that our test accepts: 73 only because of the collision flags raised during the scene reset, and 42 because the heading kept drifting after hand-off. The two scores disagree on 191 trials (16.6%). They also disagree on where the policy works best. The built-in score rates Vr = 18 m/s with ψg = 105 ◦ as the best combination, at 54.2%, while under our test no trial in that cell completes the reversal: the vehicle stops short with a median heading change of 158 ◦ and reaches 180 ◦ only by coasting after hand-off. A loose terminal-heading score of the kind used in (2) therefore does not merely overestimate success; it reverses the conclusion about which settings are safe to use. 

B. Where the Policy Fails 

Fig. 4a shows the acceptance rate for each threshold combination. Six of the twelve combinations lie entirely in F, with no accepted trial out of 96. The policy works only at moderate reverse speed: Vr = 10 m/s is accepted at every heading threshold, between 37.5% and 56.2%, while 

Vr = 6 m/s works only with ψg = 75 ◦ (43.8%) and 

Vr ≥ 14 m/s almost never works. The effect of ψg therefore reverses with Vr : a later switch helps at Vr = 10 m/s and prevents completion at Vr = 6 m/s. The boundary ∂F in (11) is not aligned with the axes, so the two thresholds interact and testing them one at a time would miss the failure structure. A development campaign that happened to test a single combination is reported between 0% and 56.2%. Fig. 4b shows that the physical parameters matter as much as the schedule. The initial state has the strongest effect. When the maneuver starts 0.5 s after the vehicle is placed, no trial is accepted under any weather, while waits of 2.5 to 6.5 s give 17.0% to 27.1%; the vehicle is most likely still settling on its suspension after placement, so the first phase starts from a state the policy never saw. Weather also changes the outcome, but not in the order friction alone would predict. Rain, with the lowest friction ( μ = 0 .6), gives 22.6%, close to Clear and Cloudy ( μ = 0 .9, 20.1%), while Fog ( μ = 0 .85 )gives only 4.9%. In this campaign the Fog trials also start with the vehicle already rolling at 0.49 m/s, so the Fog result cannot be separated from this difference in initial speed; either way, it is a change in physical condition that training did not include. The two remaining CARLA parameters, the time of day and the post-maneuver follow time, did not affect the maneuver: acceptance was 16.8% by day and 17.0% at night, and between 16.7% and 17.2% across the three follow times. They are therefore omitted from Fig. 4b, and their flat response confirms that the differences above come from the maneuver and not from the test setup. 

C. Efficiency of Adaptive Coverage 

Replaying the adaptive coverage method on the exhaustive set, with each query returning the logged outcome of that grid point, shows how much of this picture it recovers with fewer trials. The search stops after 631 trials, 54.8% of the grid: 128 at the vertices, 60 in edge bisection, 32 interior probes, and 411 in safe-zone tracing. Every level of every dimension is tested, so SCI in (13) equals 1 for any choice of weights. It finds 181 of the 195 accepted trials, all of which lie on ∂F, so R∂ = 0 .928 ; the two measures therefore differ, and only R∂ reveals what the search misses. The search recovers the main conclusions above: the four threshold combinations with acceptance of at least 37.5%, no accepted trial in the six combinations that have none (one of which, 

Vr = 18 m/s with ψg = 90 ◦, is never sampled because both of its neighbors along the edge fail), and the complete failure at a 0.5 s wait. It misses the two sparse safe islands at Vr = 14 and 18 m/s with ψg = 75 ◦, where acceptance is only 12.5% and 2.1% and all 36 and 32 sampled points failed. This is the expected limit of a vertex-first search: a small isolated region is found only if a probe happens to land in it, so denser interior probing is needed where such islands matter.

E. Sim2Real2Sim Validation 

All preceding results come from simulation, so they are only as reliable as the metrics behind them are on real hard-ware. Real tests of stunt maneuvers are themselves safety-critical: a failed J-turn on a real vehicle can end in a spin, a rollover, or a collision, so each real trial must be placed where the risk is lowest. The Sim2Real2Sim loop serves both needs. The simulation identifies safe zones, combinations of thresholds and conditions in which the maneuver is predicted to complete without approaching a dynamic limit, and real trials are run only there. The real measurements then show whether the simulated metrics that defined those zones can be trusted. We close the loop in three steps: the simulation chooses where to test, the real vehicle is measured there, and the measurements are compared with the simulator at matched conditions to find which stunt-driving indicators transfer and which are simulator artifacts.

Peak yaw rate is 40 .0◦/s measured against 208 ◦/s simulated, and the timing differs in kind: the simulated rotation is a burst of about 1.3 s within a 5.3 s run, while the real rotation is spread almost evenly over its window. Most of this gap is due to speed. The real executions peak at 5.6 m/s against 12 .8 m/s in simulation, and at a fixed path curvature yaw rate grows with speed, so rate metrics transfer only after normalization by speed, while geometric metrics transfer directly. Normalization does not reconcile the dynamic loading. As a fraction of the friction limit, the real executions reach 

0.51 , while the simulated ensemble reaches 10 .4, about 4.9 g,which no tire can produce on asphalt. The logged channels show the same defect from the opposite side (Table II): the simulator reports between 3.5 and 11 times the real peak lateral acceleration but only about one tenth of the real peak roll. These two errors cannot both be correct, because in a physical vehicle larger lateral acceleration produces larger roll through load transfer. The pattern points to the simulated suspension and load-transfer path, which appears too stiff: the chassis accelerometer records sharp lateral spikes while the body barely rolls. Neither the simulation nor the real data alone reveals this defect; it appears only when the two are compared. 

F. Interpretation and Limitations 

The drop from the reported 90.11% to the validated 16.9% has two parts. The larger part, from 90.11% to 18.7%, comes from the test conditions: the policy was refined using feedback from a single operating point, and its schedule does not carry over to other initial states, weather, and threshold settings. The smaller part, from 18.7% to 16.9%, comes from the added recoverability requirement. The general les-son is that a learned policy can be highly reliable under the parameters it was trained with and still fail under the parameters that actually change on the road, and a validation that reuses the training conditions and objective cannot detect this. The planar model omits load transfer beyond the lateral and yaw dynamics, including gyroscopic effects [9], which is the same channel the comparison found unreliable in the simulator. The weather condition with the lowest acceptance is confounded with a nonzero initial speed in this campaign, so the separate effects of fog and initial speed remain to be measured. And the test space contains a single vehicle; extending it to obstacles and perception, and adding real-vehicle trials in the low-acceptance regions identified by the simulation, is the most direct path toward a deployment argument. V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper argued that a learned maneuver policy must be validated independently of its training, with physical test parameters that training kept fixed and acceptance criteria that the training objective does not contain. Applied to G-Maneuver, a learning-based policy for stunt J-turn maneuvers whose approach reports 90.11% success, this validation found that the policy completed the reversal in 18.7% of 1,152 trials and was accepted in 16.9%. The failures were concentrated in conditions that training never varied, namely an unsettled initial state, a change in weather, and switching thresholds away from the trained values, and failed trials were more aggressive than successful ones. Since real stunt tests are safety-critical, the Sim2Real2Sim loop provides a practical way to find safe zones in which such maneuvers can be performed on real vehicles with minimum risk, and it showed that the kinematic stunt-driving indicators transfer to hardware while force and roll indicators do not. Because the protocol maps physical hazards to tests through standard safety frameworks, it provides a shared basis on which other teams can validate their own learned policies for autonomous vehicle maneuvers.
