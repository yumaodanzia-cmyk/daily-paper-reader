## Abstract 

End-to-end autonomous driving is predominantly evaluated through ego-vehicle safety and task completion. Its deploy-ment in urban traffic, however, creates sustained interac-tions among vehicles whose collective effects remain insuf-ficiently evaluated. We introduce Ego2Flow, a closed-loop benchmark for assessing the traffic-system impact of au-tonomous driving on maps distilled from real-world cities. To construct realistic 3D urban environments for sustained closed-loop traffic simulation, we develop an agent-guided map distillation framework that integrates real-world maps and observations with complementary geographic informa-tion. Through an iterative feedback mechanism and a traffic information graph that jointly guide scene generation, the framework preserves lane topology, critical road geometry and decision-relevant visual conditions while simplifying nonessential details and completing unobserved regions. Using our proposed framework, we generate and publicly release six CARLA maps from the Argoverse 2 dataset, cov-

1arXiv:2611.17720v1 [cs.RO] 13 Nov 2026 ering the corresponding real-world regions and enabling paired comparisons of driving behavior under real and sim-ulated observations. On these maps, Ego2Flow evaluates how autonomous driving policies affect traffic efficiency, safety, stability and energy consumption across diverse road structures. Compared with existing construction methods, our framework achieves the lowest average displacement error between planning outputs from paired real and simu-lated observations. ## 1. Introduction 

End-to-end autonomous driving learns policies that trans-late sensor observations into driving decisions and has emerged as a prominent paradigm in autonomous driving research [14, 21, 23, 26]. Closed-loop simulation is cen-tral to evaluating these policies because it captures the feed-back between their actions and subsequent observations, re-vealing driving behavior that open-loop prediction metrics may fail to reflect [7, 9]. Within this closed-loop setting, existing driving benchmarks primarily assess the ego ve-hicle’s ability to navigate safely and complete its assigned task [1, 3, 17]. However, urban deployment raises a further question about the traffic produced by those decisions. A policy’s ability to complete an individual trip does not es-tablish how its widespread deployment would affect urban traffic systems. Vehicle interactions carry the effects of a vehicle’s driv-ing decision beyond the controlled vehicle. For example, a vehicle’s braking can induce speed disturbances that prop-agate through a platoon and delay following vehicles. Fig-ure 1 illustrates this distinction: the same pedestrian cross-ing can induce different patterns of speed loss and spacing along a platoon, even when the leading vehicle completes the maneuver safely. Autonomous driving policies should therefore avoid amplifying such disturbances and exacer-bating delays for following vehicles. Yet evaluating ego-vehicle performance alone cannot fully capture these col-lective effects. Transferring driving control to algorithms also creates an opportunity to improve traffic-system outcomes that individual human drivers do not typically optimize for. Learning-based control has improved traffic flow in sim-ulation [33], and field experiments have shown that con-trolling a single vehicle can suppress stop-and-go waves in predominantly human-driven traffic [29]. However, exper-iments with commercial adaptive cruise control have also observed disturbance amplification [12]. These contrasting findings motivate the central question of Ego2Flow: how does a driving policy affect the traffic it joins, and does its control improve collective outcomes? 

Evaluating these effects for sensor-based driving poli-cies requires environments that preserve decision-relevant road and visual conditions while supporting sustained in-teractions among vehicles. We introduce Ego2Flow , a closed-loop benchmark for assessing the traffic-system im-pact of autonomous driving on maps distilled from real-world roads. As summarized in Fig. 1, it provides six CARLA [10] maps covering the corresponding Argoverse 2 (AV2) regions [32]. Across these diverse urban road envi-ronments, Ego2Flow assesses end-to-end driving policies through their effects on traffic efficiency, safety, stability and estimated energy consumption. To support this evaluation, we develop an agent-guided map-distillation framework for constructing 3D urban en-vironments. Distillation focuses on faithfully retaining the road structure and visual cues that shape driving decisions, while simplifying less relevant geometry and appearance in-stead of reproducing every scene detail. A traffic informa-tion graph connects these priorities to source evidence and dependencies among road elements, guiding the agent’s de-cisions about how to represent observed features and com-plete the surrounding network (Fig. 2). Static and dynamic review provide localized feedback on agreement with the recorded scene and functionality in simulation, allowing the maps to be refined for traffic evaluation. We publicly release six CARLA maps covering the cor-responding AV2 regions and connected surrounding roads, supporting joint use with AV2 data for closed-loop sim-ulation of end-to-end driving policies. Across six cities, our framework achieves the lowest mean ADE between a frozen driving model’s planning outputs under real and rendered observations, 9.3% below the next-best construc-tion method. Initial benchmarking of representative rule-based and learned driving policies reveals differences in traffic-system performance that task completion alone can-not capture. These findings establish empirical baselines and identify opportunities to improve collective traffic out-comes through driving-policy development. Our contributions are threefold: • We introduce Ego2Flow, a closed-loop benchmark that extends the evaluation of end-to-end driving policies be-yond ego-vehicle outcomes to the collective performance of urban traffic. • We develop an agent-guided map-distillation framework that prioritizes information relevant to driving decisions when constructing executable 3D urban environments. A traffic information graph informs construction, while static and dynamic feedback guide refinement. • We publicly release six CARLA maps spatially aligned with AV2, enabling paired evaluation of driving poli-cies under real and simulated observations and supporting closed-loop simulation in the realistic urban regions. 22. Related Work 

End-to-end autonomous driving. End-to-end au-tonomous driving uses a unified learning framework to map raw sensor inputs directly to vehicle motion plans or control commands. Such policies can be learned through imitation learning [23] and further refined through reinforcement learning [38]. Recent vision–language–action (VLA) models augment this mapping with language reasoning for trajectory planning [25]. World modeling can further support these policies through future scene prediction [21] and help assess navigation instructions by imagining their consequences [34]. Given the prominence of end-to-end driving, we evaluate the traffic-system effects of policies that act on sensor observations, extending beyond motion controllers operating on explicit traffic states. 

Closed-loop evaluation. Closed-loop evaluation assesses driving policies through interaction, typically in simula-tors where actions shape subsequent observations. Driv-ing benchmarks emphasize safety, progress and task com-pletion [1, 3, 17], while simulation benchmarks assess the realism of agent behavior [22]. Cooperative benchmarks examine whether perception sharing and decision negoti-ation improve the driving performance of connected ve-hicles [8, 19, 20]. At the traffic-system level, Flow [33] and freeway benchmarks [36] study mixed-autonomy con-trol and its collective effects, while IntersectionZoo [16] ex-amines the generalization of cooperative eco-driving across urban intersections. These traffic-control studies primar-ily use explicit traffic states as policy inputs in their eval-uations. Ego2Flow evaluates the traffic-system impact of sensor-based end-to-end policies in 3D urban environments that support sensor rendering and sustained vehicle interac-tions. 

Environment construction for driving simulation. Ex-isting pipelines construct explicit 3D environments from ge-ographic data and scene evidence through procedural mod-eling and asset assembly [4, 8, 28], or multimodal agent-guided generation [31]. Neural reconstruction methods learn renderable scene representations from driving logs to improve visual fidelity [35, 37], while Cam2Sim [15] inte-grates Gaussian Splatting with executable CARLA scenes. Generative models provide complementary capabilities for synthesizing road layouts [6, 27] and visual content [24]. We focus on distilling real-world scenes into CARLA maps that preserve driving-relevant features. 

## 3. Agent-Guided Map Distillation 

Map distillation converts partial road observations into exe-cutable 3D environments by retaining information that gov-erns driving and traffic interactions. Ego2Flow assigns scene detail according to lane connectivity, physical geom-etry, traffic controls, and decision-relevant visibility. As shown in Fig. 2, a traffic information graph connects these requirements to source evidence and construction tasks. The construction agent uses the graph to select which prop-erties to retain and which elements to revise. The resulting conditions guide local generation and simulator assembly, while static and dynamic feedback identify the properties requiring further revision. 

3.1. Source Alignment and Fusion 

Let O denote the available source evidence. AV2 dataset provides local lane maps, calibrated images, LiDAR, ego poses, and object annotations [32], OpenStreetMap supplies the surrounding road network and building footprints [13] and terrain data provides regional elevation. We register these sources in a common metric frame and fuse detailed AV2 geometry with the surrounding network. Roads, cam-era poses, and actor states share the same alignment, while image timestamps and calibration retain the correspondence needed for source-aligned simulation. The extended net-work supports routes and traffic interactions beyond the recorded regions. At construction iteration n = 0 , 1, . . . , the scene repre-sentation Sn contains geometry, traffic attributes, objects, and source references. Evidence is associated with individ-ual properties, allowing an observed lane boundary to re-main fixed while an inferred height or adjacent surface is refined. Fusion identifies gaps between retained road seg-ments. The positions, tangents, widths, elevations, and lane connections at their interfaces provide boundary conditions for local completion.

3.4. Static and Dynamic Feedback 

Independent review. Static and dynamic reviewers as-sess Mn+1 independently of the construction agent. Static review compares source-aligned rendered views and geo-metric measurements against O and Cn, examining connec-tivity, surface continuity, asset placement, traffic controls, visibility, and appearance. Dynamic review follows static acceptance. It combines route-following checks, learned-policy driving under sustained traffic, and replay of camera and actor states at source timestamps. These tests expose navigation failures, collisions, vehicle-response problems, and interaction failures during simulation. ## 4. Ego2Flow Benchmark 

Ego2Flow measures how a driving policy changes the broader traffic system. The evaluation unit is the traffic gen-erated by a set of travel requests over an observation win-dow. It includes vehicles controlled by the policy, surround-ing traffic and requests waiting for admission to the road network. Each scenario fixes the road network, demand, ex-ternally prescribed controls and observation window. Fig-ure 1 organizes these evaluations into six city maps with three task families in two deployment settings and four met-ric categories. A policy can affect another vehicle through car-following interactions, lane occupancy or access to an intersection. Requests waiting for admission also experi-ence the resulting delay. The benchmark accounts for all these vehicles and requests to relate driving decisions to traffic efficiency, safety, stability and estimated energy con-sumption. 

4.1. Maps and Traffic Tasks 

The six maps cover Austin, Detroit, Miami, Palo Alto, Pitts-burgh and Washington, D.C. The benchmark design allo-cates eight areas per city. Each city includes four areas with sustained traffic and two each for bottlenecks and perturba-tion propagation. Each area defines legal routes and task exits. Its traffic setup specifies initially present vehicles, incoming travel requests and measurement intervals. Up-stream and downstream road space accommodates arrivals and queues. A buffer beyond each task exit allows vehicles that have completed their trips to leave without obstructing following traffic. 

Sustained traffic. Vehicles serve a fixed sequence of travel requests over a continuous observation window. Compatible AV2 observations jointly initialize vehicle po-sitions, speeds and relative configurations. Additional re-quests specify continuing demand and scheduled release times. This task measures whether a policy can serve de-mand while maintaining safe and steady motion. 

Bottleneck response. A lane restriction or a constraint on downstream discharge reduces the road’s capacity to serve demand. The scenario fixes the arrival process as well as the restriction location and its start and end times. Continued arrivals reveal queue accumulation, and traffic recovery is assessed after the restriction is lifted. Queue and discharge time series complement the common efficiency and safety metrics. 5Perturbation propagation. This task examines how ve-hicles respond to a disturbance transmitted through their in-teractions. After an external leader performs a sharp speed reduction, a fixed set of followers is observed. Neighbor-ing traffic remains active in the simulation. Each policy determines the car-following behavior and lane occupancy through which the disturbance propagates. This task mea-sures speed losses across all vehicles to characterize the traffic system’s response to a perturbation under each pol-icy. 

4.2. Deployment and Measurement 

We use two deployment settings. Homogeneous deploy-ment (H) assigns the tested policy to all vehicles. Mixed de-ployment (M) assigns it to a fixed subset of approximately 30% of requests, with CARLA BasicAgent controlling the remaining vehicles. The external leader is controlled sep-arately. H tests the collective behavior under the repeated driving policy, whereas M evaluates interactions with the background controller. Requests, routes, vehicle properties, weather, externally prescribed controls and warm-up pro-cedures are shared across policies. Each policy shapes the traffic state at the start of observation through its interac-tions during warm-up. We evaluate all vehicles except the external leader, clip-ping trajectories to the observation window and a region extending 4 m beyond the task lane surfaces. A request is completed when its vehicle follows a valid route and its rear crosses the task exit. An illegal transition or sustained de-parture from the road corridor marks the request as failed, but the vehicle remains included in motion, safety and en-ergy measurements. Failed requests and those awaiting ad-mission accrue delay until the observation window ends, en-suring that the evaluation accounts for the full demand.

Framework Component Analysis To assess the contri-bution of each framework component, we conduct an ab-lation study on a randomly selected scene from the Palo Alto map. We compare the full framework with vari-ants that individually omit the traffic information graph, static feedback or dynamic feedback, using the same paired OneVL evaluation. As shown in Table 2, the full frame-work achieves the lowest ADE and FDE. Removing static feedback produces the largest ADE increase of 7.0%, while separately omitting dynamic feedback and the traffic infor-mation graph increases ADE by 4.9% and 2.2%, respec-tively. These comparisons support the effectiveness of each component in the proposed framework. 

5.2. Traffic-System Policy Evaluation 

We compare two rule-based baselines, CARLA Traffic Manager (TM) [5] and an IDM-based controller [30], with three end-to-end policies: UniAD [14], Alpamayo [25] and LEAD [23]. The rule-based baselines use simulator state, while the end-to-end policies act on sensor observations with fixed model weights. Closed-loop simulations use syn-chronous 0.05 s time steps and identical vehicle physics set-tings across policies. Table 3 reports normalized system-level scores aggre-gated across the applicable scenarios and both deployment settings. The IDM-based controller achieves the highest to-tal score of 71.804, followed by TM at 64.615. LEAD per-forms best among the evaluated end-to-end policies, with a total score of 54.215. However, the overall ranking con-ceals differences across individual metrics, with no policy achieving the best score in every dimension. 

Performance across metrics. Traffic efficiency, safety, stability and energy consumption capture different aspects of system-level performance. A policy need not achieve the best value in every metric and policy development can instead seek Pareto-efficient trade-offs that reflect deploy-ment priorities. The IDM-based controller leads in effi-ciency, low-TTC, braking and peak loss scores, while TM achieves the best collision and energy scores. Among the end-to-end policies, UniAD achieves the highest speed SD score among all evaluated policies. Compared with LEAD, it scores higher on stability metrics but lower in the other dimensions. These differences reveal strengths and weak-nesses that individual metrics cannot fully describe, provid-ing directions for policy improvement beyond ego-vehicle 8performance. 

Overall performance. The total score summarizes per-formance across metrics, while the failure fraction records the proportion of runs that fail the evaluation checks, includ-ing those with negligible vehicle movement or insufficient leader speed loss. The rule-based baselines lead most of the individual metric rankings and achieve higher total scores than the evaluated end-to-end policies. These results do not negate their reported ego-level driving capabilities, for example, LEAD achieves strong performance on ego-level closed-loop benchmarks such as Bench2Drive [23]. The contrast suggests that strong ego-level performance does not necessarily translate into better traffic-system outcomes. Jointly assessing ego-level and system-level performance can therefore reveal limitations that individual-vehicle eval-uation may overlook and guide policy development toward improved urban traffic performance. 

## 6. Conclusion 

We presented Ego2Flow, a closed-loop benchmark for as-sessing how end-to-end driving policies affect the traf-fic they join. Our agent-guided map-distillation frame-work constructs connected 3D environments by preserv-ing driving-relevant road structure and visual cues while simplifying nonessential detail. Paired evaluations demon-strate strong planning-output fidelity, with the lowest mean ADE among the compared construction methods. Bench-marking representative policies across 48 scenarios reveals trade-offs among traffic efficiency, safety, stability and en-ergy consumption, showing that more sophisticated driv-ing models do not necessarily yield better traffic-flow per-formance. We publicly release six CARLA maps spa-tially aligned with AV2, supporting paired evaluation and closed-loop simulation in the corresponding urban regions. These resources and empirical findings provide a basis for developing driving policies that account for their effects on surrounding traffic and improve collective outcomes.
