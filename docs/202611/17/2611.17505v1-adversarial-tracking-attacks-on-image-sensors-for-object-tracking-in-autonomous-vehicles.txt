# Adversarial Tracking: Attacks on Image Sensors for Object Tracking in Autonomous Vehicles 

Han Wu, Alex Chan, Ekin Can Erkus, Tousif Rahman and Rishad Shafik 

School of Engineering, Newcastle University, Newcastle upon Tyne, United Kingdom 

Corresponding author: han.wu2@newcastle.ac.uk 

Abstract —Object tracking systems estimate the motion of sur-rounding vehicles by linking detected objects across video frames. In particular, Tracking-By-Detection (TBD) systems combine object detectors with motion prediction and data association to maintain object identities over time. However, neural network-based detectors are vulnerable to adversarial attacks that per-turb input images, and their impact on downstream trajectory estimation is not fully characterised by detection accuracy alone. Therefore, this paper evaluates image-specific and image-agnostic attacks on YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 detectors paired with SORT, Deep SORT, Strong SORT, and OC-SORT tracking systems. The evaluation uses CARLA simulator and measures tracking quality through Multiple Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA), Higher Order Tracking Accuracy (HOTA), and Identification F1 (IDF1). Under image-specific attacks, HOTA for YOLOv3-based trackers declines from 53.45–56.19% to 14.85–17.03%, while MOTA reaches -772.60% in the worst case. YOLOv4-based trackers exhibit greater robustness, while HOTA still declines to 25.06–26.86%. In addition, image-agnostic attacks can reduce the tracking accuracy using a single Universal Adversarial Perturbation (UAP), without generating a separate perturbation for individual frames. 

Index Terms —Edge Sensors, Autonomous Vehicles, Object Tracking, Adversarial Attacks 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Object tracking is a fundamental perception task in au-tonomous driving that estimates the locations and identities of surrounding objects over time [1]. Camera-based object tracking includes Single-Object Tracking (SOT) and Multi-Object Tracking (MOT). SOT follows one specified target, whereas MOT follows several objects and maintains a separate tracklet for each [2]. Although deep neural networks have substantially advanced the tracking performance, they are vulnerable to adversarial attacks that perturb input images [3]. For example, Tracking-By-Detection (TBD) systems combine neural object detectors with motion prediction and association across consecutive frames. Some TBD systems rely on pre-trained object detec-tors, and perturbations generated against widely used object detection models, such as YOLO [4]–[6], may transfer to tracking systems. Therefore, the tracking performance of TBD systems heavily rely on the accuracy of object detectors. However, detection errors do not necessarily translate directly into tracking errors, as the data association stage may reject inconsistent detections [3]. Nevertheless, persistent detection errors can disrupt data association, leading to fragmented trajectories [7], as shown in Fig. 1.

A. Object Tracking 

Camera-based Multi-Object Tracking (MOT) methods gen-erally follow two approaches, Joint Detection and Tracking (JDT) and Tracking-By-Detection (TBD). JDT methods in-tegrate detection and tracking, whereas TBD methods use a modular framework with an object detector, state estimation, an optional feature extractor, and data association [1]. Fig-ure 2 summarizes these components and their roles in online tracking. 

Object Detector: The object detector identifies objects in each camera frame and provides bounding boxes, confidence scores, and predicted classes. The tracking stages then associate detections across frames to maintain object identities. 

State Estimation : To maintain object identities, SORT-like trackers commonly use a Kalman filter to predict the next state of each track [9]. Under a constant-velocity model, the state is represented by 

x = [ u, v, s, r, ˙u, ˙v, ˙s]T (1) where (u, v ) is the box center, s is its scale, r is its aspect ratio, and [ ˙ u, ˙v, ˙s] denotes the corresponding rates. The Kalman filter updates this state when a detection is associated with the predicted track. Consequently, association can reject isolated inconsistent boxes, but a sustained series of false detections may still form incorrect trajectories. 

Feature Extractor (Optional) : Motion state alone may be insufficient to distinguish nearby or occluded objects. There-fore, appearance-based trackers, including Deep SORT, use a convolutional neural network (CNN) to extract descriptors that support identity association [10]. This optional stage is shown in Fig. 2. 

Data Association: The data association module matches cur-rent detections to predicted track states and, when available, image-derived features. The Mahalanobis distance [13] can measure the discrepancy between a detection and a predicted state. Subsequently, the Hungarian algorithm [14] can solve the resulting assignment problem. Although these stages help maintain object identities, their effectiveness depends on the incoming detections.

C. Adversarial Attacks 

Both JDT and TBD methods have been studied under adversarial attacks. For example, attacks on GOTURN-style single-object tracking [18] and FairMOT [19] alter learned tracking behavior. For modular TBD systems, an attacker may instead target the detector, the association process, or tracklet management. The distinction matters because detector-only attacks need not access the internal tracking model. 

Attacking Object Detectors : The accuracy of TBD meth-ods heavily relies on the object detector’s ability to produce precise bounding boxes of target objects. Missed or misplaced detections can therefore disrupt subsequent track association. In 2019, the first adversarial attack against MOT models at-tacked a TBD method that used YOLOv3 as the object detector [3]. Moreover, YOLOX [6] and CenterNet [20] stand out as two widely-used object detectors for autonomous driving applications, yet they both exhibit vulnerability to adversarial attacks [21], [22]. To mitigate adversarial attacks, temporal outlier detection offers a potential approach to identifying adversarial perturbations [23]. 

Attacking Association Methods : Association assigns an identity to each detected object across frames. Liu et al. [24] describe attacks that alter object-label evidence and can undermine association. Unlike those methods, detector-only attacks first alter the image measurements passed to otherwise unchanged tracking modules. 

Attacking Tracklets : Tracklets retain an object’s estimated position and motion, often through Kalman-filter state pre-diction. An error in these observations can alter subsequent matches and, in some cases, switch nearby identities. Lin et al. [7] attacked the appearance and distance cues of neighbor-ing pedestrians to disrupt tracking. Such attacks differ from the detector-input perturbations considered here because they explicitly seek to affect temporal association. The evaluation investigates whether conventional tracking components, such as the Kalman filter and the Hungarian algorithm, can mitigate the effects of adversarial attacks. A. Image-Specific PCB Attack 

The image-specific attack was tested with hyperparameters of attack strength ϵ = 8 and learning rate decay k = 0 .99 . We updated the perturbation frame-by-frame, iterating one step per frame for 300 consecutive frames on each of the 7 maps. After applying adversarial perturbations, MOTA decreases significantly for both YOLOv3 and YOLOv4, as shown in Fig. 3. YOLOv3 generates many small false-positive boxes, whereas YOLOv4 produces fewer but larger spurious detec-tions (see Fig. 3 (a) and (b)). Notably, the MOTAs of YOLOv3 drop to negative values due to a large number of false positives. The effectiveness of the image-specific attack can be further improved by increasing the number of optimization iterations per frame, as false positives are relatively rare in the initial frames, leaving the tracking method largely unaffected. HOTA and IDF1 also decline, as shown in the retained Appendix plots, Figs. 5 and 6. These findings demonstrate that perturbing the object detection model alone is sufficient to compromise TBD frameworks. 

B. Image-Agnostic Attack 

The image-agnostic attack generates an Universal Adversar-ial Perturbation (UAP) to attack all video frames. We trained the UAP on Map 02 for 100 iterations, as Map 02 generalizes best across all maps. The training was conducted with a learning rate of α = 0 .001 , a learning rate decay of k = 0 .98 ,and an attack strength of ϵ = 8 .Figure 4 shows that the UAP reduces MOTA for both detectors. For YOLOv3, this reduction is smaller than that under the image-specific attack. However, several YOLOv4 combinations show a larger MOTA reduction with the UAP, so the relative effect depends on the detector. The HOTA and IDF1 results in Figs. 5 and 6 provide corresponding evidence of degraded track association and identity preservation. The YOLOv3 and YOLOv4 examples predominantly con-tain false detections labeled as birds and chairs, respectively, as shown in Fig. 4(a) and (b). Vehicle-class filtering can reject many such boxes, but the resulting MOTA losses show that downstream tracking is not fully protected. These examples do not establish how much of the remaining error comes from missed vehicles or accepted false tracks. Vehicle-targeted perturbations would therefore provide a useful further compar-ison. V. C ONCLUSION 

This study evaluated four real-time online tracking methods—SORT, Deep SORT, Strong SORT, and OC SORT—using the CARLA dataset. With the same object detector, OC SORT achieved tracking accuracy comparable to Strong SORT without requiring additional neural networks for feature extraction. This finding suggests that improving conventional tracking components, such as the Kalman filter, can enhance tracking accuracy without introducing additional deep learning-based components and potential attack surfaces. Furthermore, for TBD methods, the tracking performance is highly dependent on detection accuracy. Our experimen-tal results demonstrated that both image-specific and image-agnostic attacks substantially degraded MOTA, HOTA and IDF1, indicating that perturbing the object detector alone is sufficient to compromise the tracking system.
