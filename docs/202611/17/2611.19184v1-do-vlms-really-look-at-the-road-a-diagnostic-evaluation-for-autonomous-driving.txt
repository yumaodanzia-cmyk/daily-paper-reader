## Do VLMs Really Look at the Road? 

## A Diagnostic Evaluation for Autonomous Driving 

Shankar Gangisetty ∗ , Muskan Singh ∗ , and C.V. Jawahar 

International Institute of Information Technology, Hyderabad, India 

Abstract. Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly being eval-uated for autonomous driving through question-answering benchmarks, yet high benchmark accuracy does not necessarily reflect visually grounded reasoning. We present a diagnostic evaluation framework that system-atically probes whether VLMs base safety-critical driving decisions on visual evidence or instead rely on language priors, answer biases, and dataset regularities. Our benchmark comprises two task categories, Ad-verse Weather and Junctions & Intersections, with a nuScenes spatial reasoning extension, and evaluates six open-source VLMs along four diagnostic protocols: image versus no-image evaluation, counterfactual weather sensitivity, linguistic robustness under paraphrasing and nega-tion, and spatial reasoning. All ground-truth labels are derived pro-grammatically from BDD100K and DriveLM annotations, enabling re-producible evaluation without manual labeling. Across all models, we find that competitive overall accuracy conceals systematic failure modes, including strong affirmation bias, limited sensitivity to counterfactual scene changes, near-chance performance on negated questions despite high paraphrase consistency, and confident driving decisions even with-out visual input. These results demonstrate that current open-source VLMs remain insufficiently grounded for safety-critical autonomous driv-ing and underscore the need for diagnostic evaluation beyond aggregate accuracy. The code will be made publically available. 

Keywords: Vision-Language Models · Autonomous Driving · Diagnos-tic Benchmark · Visual Grounding · Counterfactual Reasoning 

1 Introduction 

Vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly being explored as interpretable reasoning modules for autonomous driving, enabling systems to answer natural-language questions about road scenes, explain driving decisions, and support interactive perception, prediction, and planning. Recent benchmarks such as DriveLM [14] and DriveBench [16] demonstrate the potential of language-guided interfaces for evaluating and interacting with driving systems. Despite this promise, strong per-formance on driving benchmarks does not necessarily imply that a model is reasoning from visual evidence. Recent studies have shown that VLMs can fail surprisingly simple visual reasoning tasks despite competitive benchmark accuracy [11]. Other work attributes this behavior to modality imbalance, where models rely disproportionately on language priors instead of image con-tent [5,13]. In autonomous driving, such reliance is particularly concerning: amodel may recommend braking, yield-ing, or switching on headlights because these actions are statistically associated with the question rather than because it has correctly recognized pedestri-ans, traffic signals, weather conditions, or other scene-specific cues. Consistent with this observation, DriveBench [16] reports that modern VLMs continue to produce plausible driving responses even when visual inputs are degraded or entirely absent. These findings suggest that aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient to assess the reliability of VLMs for safety-critical driving (Fig. 1). Reliable driving assistants must also remain robust to natural language vari-ation. Driving questions frequently involve negation and polarity, for example whether a pedestrian is not present, headlights are not required, or a junction is unsafe to enter. Prior work shows that VLMs perform only at or near chance on visually grounded negation tasks [1, 18], while exhibiting an affirmation bias, i.e., predicting the queried object or condition even when explicitly negated [6]. In autonomous driving, such failures translate directly into safety risks: unwar-ranted affirmative responses may hallucinate hazards, whereas incorrect negative responses may suppress genuine warnings. Together, these observations moti-vate evaluation protocols that test not only perception accuracy but also visual grounding and semantic robustness. Motivated by these limitations, we ask a different question from existing driving benchmarks: 

Do current VLMs genuinely reason from visual evidence when answering safety-critical driving questions, or do they rely on language priors, answer bi-ases, and dataset regularities?

2 Diagnostic Driving Evaluation Framework 

Fig. 2 provides an overview of the proposed diagnostic driving evaluation frame-work. We first describe the benchmark construction, including data curation, programmatic ground-truth derivation, and question design for the Adverse Weather and Junctions & Intersections tasks. We then present the diagnostic evaluation protocol and metrics for assessing visual grounding, counterfactual robustness, linguistic robustness, and spatial reasoning. 

2.1 Data Curation 

The benchmark comprises 520 images in total: 320 from BDD100K (including 72 counterfactual weather images) and 200 CAM_FRONT keyframes from Driv-eLM/nuScenes. This yields 26,788 binary question–answer pairs. More detailed statistics are provided in the Supplementary Material. 

BDD100K. The primary benchmark is constructed from the BDD100K valida-tion split [17]. Images are manually curated using an interactive stratified grid viewer that groups candidate frames by driving condition, enabling balanced se-lection while avoiding visually redundant scenes. For the Adverse Weather cat-egory, 108 images are selected across five conditions (26 rainy, 16 foggy, 25 snowy, 17 night-time, and 24 clear-daytime scenes). For the Junctions & Intersec-tions category, 140 images are selected across five junction types (26 signalised, 28 multi-signal, 27 pedestrian-heavy, 35 unsignalised, and 24 highway-merge in-tersections). In total, the benchmark contains 248 uniquely curated BDD100K images with balanced coverage of diverse driving scenarios. 

nuScenes and DriveLM. To extend the Junctions & Intersections bench-mark beyond scene-level reasoning, we additionally sample 200 CAM_FRONT keyframes from the DriveLM v1.1 annotations [14] on the nuScenes dataset [4]. The subset is stratified over intersection scenarios and pedestrian presence, while restricting evaluation to the front camera to leverage its camera-attributed 2D object annotations. These keyframes support spatial, predictive, and planning-oriented reasoning tasks that complement the BDD100K benchmark. 

2.2 Ground-Truth Derivation 

Ground truth is generated entirely through deterministic functions operating on existing dataset annotations, eliminating manual question-answer labelling. Each question is associated with a derivation function that maps annotation at-tributes to a binary decision label, ensuring reproducible and scalable benchmark construction. For BDD100K, derivation functions operate on enriched image annotations including weather, time of day, scene type, traffic-light states, per-class object annotations, bounding-box geometry, and occlusion metadata. 2.3 Question Design 

Questions are formulated from the driver’s perspective , requiring models to make safety-critical driving decisions rather than merely describe the scene (e.g., 

Should the driver turn on the headlights?” instead of Is it raining?” ). Each ques-tion is associated with a deterministic ground-truth derivation function, and the correct answer is designed to vary across visually similar scenes, preventing mod-els from relying solely on dataset statistics or condition labels. The benchmark comprises two complementary category tasks: Adverse Weather and Junc-tions & Intersections , each constructed to probe different aspects of visually grounded driving reasoning. 1. Adverse Weather. This task category evaluates whether models adapt driving decisions to changing weather conditions rather than relying on fixed language or safety priors. 

– Language-prior probing. Every binary driver-centric question is eval-uated under both image-conditioned and no-image settings. Comparing the two directly measures whether a model genuinely uses visual evi-dence or continues to produce plausible driving decisions when visual information is absent. 

– Counterfactual weather augmentation. To evaluate visual ground-ing, each clear-weather scene is paired with counterfactual fog, rain, and 6 S. Gangisetty et al. 

snow variants generated from the same underlying image. Fog and rain are synthesized using the physics-based degradation models of JarvisIR [9], while snow is generated using Stable Diffusion image-to-image synthe-sis [12]. Since only the weather condition changes while scene geometry remains fixed, answer changes can be attributed directly to weather-dependent visual reasoning. 

– Semantic question types. Questions are organised into five semantic types. Together, these span progressively more challenging reasoning lev-els, from recognizing visual cues to making safety-critical decisions that integrate multiple scene attributes. 

• Perception : recognising traffic lights, vehicles, pedestrians, or visibil-ity cues. 

• Action : driver decisions such as headlights, speed adaptation, or over-taking. 

• Hazard : identifying safety-critical risks arising from weather or oc-clusions. 

• Scene : understanding road, lighting, and environmental conditions. 

• Adversarial : combining multiple conditions or contrastive cues to probe deeper reasoning. 

– Linguistic variations. Every question is further expanded into two meaning-preserving paraphrases and one negated variant. This separates robustness to superficial rewording from the ability to correctly reason under semantic polarity reversal. 2. Junctions & Intersections. This category task evaluates reasoning in com-plex intersection scenarios where safe driving depends on understanding dy-namic interactions among traffic participants. 

– Language-prior probing. As in Adverse Weather, every question is evaluated with and without the driving image, exposing whether inter-section decisions remain visually grounded or are dominated by language and safety priors. 

– Semantic question types. The BDD100K benchmark includes ques-tions covering traffic-light interpretation, pedestrian awareness, hazard recognition, driver actions, and overall scene understanding using the same five semantic categories defined for Adverse Weather. 

– nuScenes spatial reasoning extension. To probe reasoning beyond scene recognition, the benchmark is extended using DriveLM/nuScenes annotations with three additional reasoning types: 

• Spatial Reasoning : relative positions of surrounding vehicles and pedes-trians (left/right/front). 

• Prediction : anticipated behavior of surrounding traffic participants. 

• Planning : ego-vehicle decisions conditioned on the spatial configura-tion of the scene. These questions require relational reasoning over object-level anno-tations rather than simple object recognition, making them substan-tially more diagnostic of higher-level driving intelligence. 2.4 Model Evaluation and Diagnostic Protocol 

The proposed framework evaluates VLMs in two stages. The first measures large-scale binary decision performance across the complete benchmark, while the sec-ond investigates why models fail through targeted reasoning analysis. Together, these stages separate quantitative performance from qualitative failure diagnosis. 

Primary Evaluation. 

VLMs are first evaluated on the complete benchmark using a constrained binary prompt that requires either Yes or No as the response. This stage pro-vides a controlled and comparable evaluation across all benchmark tasks while minimizing the influence of verbose generation styles. Responses that cannot be resolved into binary decisions are recorded as unclear and excluded from accu-racy computation. Primary evaluation is performed under four diagnostic protocols designed to expose different failure modes beyond aggregate accuracy. 

– with Image. Models receive the driving image together with a binary driv-ing question. This represents the standard evaluation setting and measures decision accuracy when visual evidence is available. 

– without Image. The driving image is replaced with a blank input while the question remains unchanged. Comparing performance between with and without image quantifies the extent to which predictions depend on visual evidence rather than language or safety priors. 

– Counterfactual weather evaluation. For Adverse Weather, every clear-weather image is paired with synthesized fog, rain, and snow variants while keeping the underlying scene unchanged. Differences in predictions reveal whether driving decisions appropriately respond to controlled visual changes instead of remaining invariant. 

– Linguistic variation evaluation. Each question is further evaluated using two paraphrases and one negated formulation. Paraphrases assess robust-ness to meaning-preserving rewording, whereas negated questions evaluate polarity-aware reasoning under semantic reversal. For the Junctions & Intersections benchmark, we additionally evaluate a nuScenes spatial reasoning extension, containing spatial reasoning, prediction, and plan-ning questions derived from DriveLM annotations. This extension examines whether models generalize beyond scene recognition to higher-level relational reasoning required for autonomous driving. 

Targeted Reasoning Evaluation. 8 S. Gangisetty et al. 

Binary predictions alone provide limited insight into the causes of model failures. We therefore perform a second evaluation stage on a failure-driven sub-set identified from the primary evaluation. This targeted analysis is conducted on representative VLMs, selected to represent different performance and bias characteristics. Instead of binary responses, models are prompted to produce a binary decision followed by a short evidence-based explanation describing the visual cues supporting their answer. By comparing explanations across image-conditioned and no-image settings, as well as across counterfactual and linguistic variants, this stage reveals whether failures arise from missing visual grounding, reliance on language or safety priors, hallucinated evidence, or incorrect semantic reasoning. Rather than relying solely on aggregate accuracy, the benchmark reports diagnostic metrics that quantify different aspects of model reliability discussed in Supplementary Material. 

3 Experimental Results 

3.1 Implementation Details 

We evaluate six representative open-source VLMs: Moondream 2B [15], SmolVLM 2.2B [10], PaliGemma 3B [3], Qwen2.5-VL 7B [2], InternVL3 8B [19], LLaVA-OV 8B [7]. All models are evaluated in a strictly zero-shot setting without task-specific fine-tuning, in-context examples, or additional prompt engineering. Inference is performed using Hugging Face transformers library with models loaded in bfloat16 precision on a single NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU. Greedy decoding is used throughout, with a maximum generation length of 50 tokens for the primary binary evaluation and 200 tokens for the targeted reasoning analysis. Binary predictions are extracted using a prefix scan for yes /no , followed by a keyword-based fallback over the first generated sentence. Responses that cannot be unambiguously mapped to either class are recorded as unclear and excluded from quantitative analysis. Due to restrictive safety guardrails, PaliGemma-3B and SmolVLM-2.2B are excluded from targeted reasoning analysis.

3.4 Qualitative Analysis of Reasoning Failures 

To understand why VLMs fail, we perform a targeted reasoning analysis on representative failure cases using evidence-based prompts (see Supplementary Material). Across both benchmark tasks, correct explanations consistently ref-erence observable scene evidence, whereas incorrect explanations are dominated by generic safety heuristics and hallucinated scene attributes rather than image-grounded observations. The same behavior persists without images, where mod-els often produce confident driving recommendations instead of acknowledging insufficient visual information. These qualitative findings explain the strong no-image performance observed quantitatively and confirm that many failures stem from reliance on language priors rather than visual grounding. Linguistic perturbations further expose poor semantic robustness: negated ques-tions are often interpreted identically to their affirmative counterparts, while meaning-preserving paraphrases can unexpectedly reverse model decisions. 4 Conclusion 

We presented a diagnostic evaluation framework for assessing whether vision-language models make safety-critical driving decisions from visual evidence or from language priors. Rather than relying on aggregate accuracy, our frame-work evaluates visual grounding through four evaluation protocols and metrics: image versus no-image evaluation, counterfactual weather sensitivity, linguistic robustness, and spatial reasoning. Across six representative open-source VLMs evaluated on BDD100K and a DriveLM/nuScenes spatial reasoning extension, we find that competitive benchmark accuracy consistently masks systematic failure modes. Our analysis reveals strong reliance on language priors, limited sensitiv-ity to counterfactual scene changes, persistent affirmation and negation biases, and confident decisions even without visual input. These findings suggest that current open-source VLMs remain insufficiently grounded for safety-critical au-tonomous driving despite producing plausible natural-language explanations. We hope this benchmark encourages future research to move beyond aggre-gate accuracy toward evaluations that explicitly measure visual grounding, ro-bustness, and reasoning reliability, ultimately enabling more trustworthy vision-language models for autonomous driving.
